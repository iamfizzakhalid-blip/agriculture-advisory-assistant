On splitting open a cane, an alcoholic smell is emitted, and the tissues are found to be reddened in one or a few internodes, usually towards the base. The reddening is most intense in the vascular bundles but extends to the pith. Often definite red blotches with a white centre, transversely elongated, are found, which are quite characteristic of the disease. Sometimes the reddening is confined to one or a few internodes in the centre of the cane, and the upper and lower parts remain free from disease. As the disease progresses the pith gradually dries up and changes to brown colour. The longitudinal depressions are formed on the rind and canes become very light and easily broken. Cavities develop in the pith, usually containing a dense growth of mycelium. When the canes are dried, the black dot-like acervuli are formed on the rind. The acervuli are formed just above or below the nodes, or in the sunken areas on the internodes. Conspicuous symptoms also appear on the mid-rib of leaves. A dark-reddish area develops on the mid-rib; it soon elongates and a blood-red lesion with dark margins develops. In mature lesions the centre becomes straw-coloured, and black dot-like acervuli develop in it. The red rot of sugarcane is caused by Colletotrichum falcatum Wint. The perfect stage is known as Physalospora tucumanensis Spegazzini, of class Ascomycetes, order Sphaeriales, family Mycosphaerellaceae. The mycelium of the fungus is inter-cellular and chiefly formed in the parenchymatous cells of the pith. The hyphae are thin, hyaline, septate, profusely branched, and contain oil globules.
