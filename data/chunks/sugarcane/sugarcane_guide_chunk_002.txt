Sugarcane propagates vegetatively rather than typically by true seed in commercial cultivation - each node on a stem cutting (sett) carries a bud that can sprout into a new shoot, and this vegetative propagation is the standard commercial planting method, since it reproduces the desired variety's characteristics reliably, unlike seed propagation which is used mainly in breeding programs to develop new varieties. The crop tillers profusely from the base, meaning a single planted sett can give rise to multiple stalks (a "stool" or clump) over the growing season. Sugarcane flowers (produces a plume-like inflorescence called an "arrow") under certain day-length and climatic conditions, but flowering is generally undesirable in commercial cane production since it can reduce sugar content and cane growth, and many commercial varieties are selected partly for reduced or delayed flowering tendency. TYPES AND CLASSIFICATION - Noble canes (Saccharum officinarum): Traditional thick-stalked, high-sucrose canes, generally lower yielding and more disease-susceptible than modern hybrids, now rarely grown commercially on their own but historically important as parents in breeding programs. - Modern commercial hybrid varieties (Saccharum spp. hybrids): The vast majority of commercially grown sugarcane today consists of complex hybrids bred from crosses among Saccharum officinarum and related wild/related species (such as Saccharum spontaneum, valued for vigor, disease resistance, and ratooning ability), combining high sugar content with strong yield, disease resistance, and adaptability. - Varieties are further distinguished by maturity group (early, mid, and late maturing), which affects the timing of peak sucrose content and is used by mills to plan a staggered harvest/crushing schedule across the season.
