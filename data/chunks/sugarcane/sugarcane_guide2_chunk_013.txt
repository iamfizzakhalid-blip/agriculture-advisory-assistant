Various leaf diseases, including several fungal leaf spots and rusts, can also reduce photosynthetic leaf area, though they are generally less destructive than red rot, smut, or ratoon stunting disease. Because so many of the most serious sugarcane diseases spread through infected planting material, using certified, disease-free seed cane, treating setts before planting, and avoiding the use of visibly diseased stalks as seed cane are among the most important disease prevention practices in the crop. Harvesting sugarcane is timed around peak sucrose accumulation rather than a single fixed physiological maturity marker like the black layer stage in maize, and farmers and mills typically judge readiness using a combination of visual cues, such as a change in stalk color, drying and yellowing of the lower leaves, and reduced vertical growth, along with actual sugar content testing using instruments that measure brix, meaning the concentration of dissolved solids largely made up of sucrose in the juice squeezed from a sample stalk; cane is generally harvested once this reading indicates that sucrose content has reached its economically optimal peak, since harvesting too early sacrifices potential sugar yield and harvesting too late can allow sugar content to decline again as the plant's stored sucrose begins to be used for other purposes or lost through natural processes late in the season. Harvesting itself involves cutting the stalks close to ground level, traditionally done by hand with a machete-like tool, which remains common in many smallholder and labor-abundant regions, or increasingly by mechanical harvesters in larger, more mechanized operations, which cut, strip leaves, and chop the cane into shorter billets in a single pass.
