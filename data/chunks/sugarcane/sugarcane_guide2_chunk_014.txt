Timing of harvest relative to ratooning is also important, since cutting the stalks cleanly and close to the ground, without excessively damaging the stubble left behind, directly affects how well the field will regrow as a ratoon crop afterward. Unlike grain crops such as maize, rice, or wheat, sugarcane has essentially no long-term on-farm storage step, because the sugar content of harvested cane begins to decline fairly quickly once the stalks are cut, due to ongoing respiration and the activity of naturally occurring microorganisms that begin breaking down sucrose in the cut stalk; because of this, harvested cane needs to be transported and processed at a sugar mill as quickly as possible after cutting, ideally within about twenty four to forty eight hours, to avoid meaningful losses in recoverable sugar, which is quite different from crops like maize or rice where proper drying allows the grain to be stored safely for months. At the mill, the harvested cane is crushed to extract the juice, which is then clarified, boiled down, and crystallized through a multi-step industrial process to produce raw and eventually refined sugar, with the fibrous leftover material after juice extraction, called bagasse, commonly burned as fuel to power the mill itself or used to make paper products, and molasses, a thick byproduct syrup left after as much sugar as practical has been crystallized out, used in products ranging from animal feed to the production of ethanol and rum. Beyond raw and refined sugar, sugarcane has a number of other significant uses.
