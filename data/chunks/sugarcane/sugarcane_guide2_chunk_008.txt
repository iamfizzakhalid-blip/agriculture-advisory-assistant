Ratooning is attractive to farmers because it avoids the substantial cost and labor of land preparation and replanting for each cycle, but ratoon crops generally yield somewhat less than the original plant cane crop, and this yield decline tends to become more pronounced with each successive ratoon, partly due to a gradual buildup of pests, diseases, and soil-borne problems in a field that is not disturbed by plowing between cycles, and partly due to gradual depletion of the stubble's vigor over repeated cutting cycles. Because of this, most farmers plan to ratoon a field for a limited number of cycles, commonly somewhere around two to four ratoons depending on variety, management, and how well yields are holding up, before finally replanting with fresh setts to reset the cycle and restore full yield potential. Nutrient management in sugarcane must account for the crop's very large biomass and long growing cycle, which means it removes substantially more total nutrients from the soil over its cycle than most annual crops, even though nutrient application is often expressed per year to allow comparison. Nitrogen is again the most critical nutrient, with typical total applications for a full plant cane cycle often falling somewhere in the range of150 to 250 kilograms per hectare depending on region, variety, and yield target, generally split into several doses applied at planting, during early tillering, and again during the early part of the grand growth phase, since heavy nitrogen application too late in the season can actually delay ripening and reduce sugar accumulation by keeping the plant in a vegetative rather than sugar-storing mode.
