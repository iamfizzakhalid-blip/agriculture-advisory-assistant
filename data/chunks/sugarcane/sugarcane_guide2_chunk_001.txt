Sugarcane, scientifically belonging to the genus Saccharum, most commonly Saccharum officinarum and its hybrids, is a tall, perennial grass grown primarily for the sugar stored in its thick stalks, though it is also used for biofuel, fodder, and various byproducts. Unlike maize and rice, which are annual crops grown from seed each season and harvested once, sugarcane is a semi-perennial crop, meaning a single planting can be harvested multiple times over several years through a process called ratooning, which will be explained in detail further down. Sugarcane is also a C4 plant like maize, meaning it photosynthesizes very efficiently in hot, sunny conditions, and this is part of why it can accumulate such large amounts of sugar in its stalk over a long growing season. The plant consists of a clump of thick, jointed stalks called canes that can grow anywhere from about two to six meters tall depending on variety and growing conditions, with each stalk divided into nodes and internodes; the nodes are the slightly swollen joints where a bud, a leaf, and root primordia are located, and the internodes are the sections between nodes where sugar accumulates in the form of sucrose stored in the stalk tissue. The leaves are long, blade-like, and arranged alternately along the stalk, wrapping around it at the base with a sheath, and as the plant grows and lower leaves age and dry out, they are usually stripped or naturally fall away, a process farmers refer to as detrashing when done manually.
