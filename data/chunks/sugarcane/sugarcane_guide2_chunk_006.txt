Incorporating farmyard manure or compost into the soil before planting, similar to practice in maize and rice, helps build organic matter and supports the crop's fertility needs over its long growing cycle. Planting sugarcane is quite different from planting maize, rice, or wheat, because it is not grown from true seed in commercial practice but rather from vegetative cuttings called setts, which are sections of mature, healthy cane stalk, typically containing two or three buds each, cut from a specially maintained seed cane crop that is kept relatively young and disease-free specifically to supply planting material. These setts are laid horizontally end to end or slightly overlapping in the bottom of the prepared furrows and then covered with a shallow layer of soil, after which the buds on each sett sprout upward to establish the new crop, essentially the same way a potato grows from a planted tuber piece rather than from true botanical seed. The amount of seed cane needed per unit area is fairly substantial compared to the seed rates of cereal crops, since whole cane stalks rather than small seeds are being used, and farmers typically need a meaningful portion of their own harvest, or a dedicated seed cane plot, set aside each year to provide planting material for the next crop cycle if they intend to replant rather than ratoon. Before planting, setts are sometimes treated by dipping them briefly in hot water or a fungicide
