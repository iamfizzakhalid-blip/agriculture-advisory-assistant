It is a major feedstock for ethanol production in some countries, notably as a large-scale biofuel source, where cane juice or molasses is fermented and distilled into ethanol used either as a fuel additive or, in some countries, as the dominant transport fuel in a blended or standalone form. Bagasse, besides being burned as mill fuel, is also used in the manufacture of paper, particleboard, and other fiber-based products. The tops and leaves of the cane plant, along with some of the trash generated during harvest, are commonly used as livestock fodder in many farming regions. Jaggery and other traditional unrefined or minimally processed sugar products, made by boiling down cane juice without the full industrial refining process used for white sugar, remain widely produced and consumed in many parts of the world, particularly in South Asia. From a farming and economic perspective, sugarcane requires a considerably larger upfront investment and a much longer time to first return than most annual crops, since the plant crop cycle alone can take over a year before the first harvest, and the substantial cost of seed cane, land preparation, and the labor involved in planting setts by hand all represent significant early expenses. However, because a single planting can then be ratooned for several additional harvests without needing to be replanted each time, the effective cost per harvest tends to average down over the full multi-year cycle of a plant crop followed by its ratoons, which is a major part of sugarcane's economic appeal compared to crops that must be fully replanted every single season.
