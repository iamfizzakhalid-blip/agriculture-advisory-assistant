Before planting, setts are sometimes treated by dipping them briefly in hot water or a fungicide solution to kill pathogens that might be present on the surface of the cane and reduce the risk of disease being carried into the new crop through the planting material itself. Planting time is generally chosen so that the germination and early tillering phases coincide with warm temperatures and adequate moisture, and specific planting windows vary a great deal by region and climate. One of the most distinctive aspects of sugarcane farming, and one that directly relates to a common point of confusion for farmers, is the difference between plant cane and ratoon cane. Plant cane refers to the crop grown from freshly planted setts, as just described, representing the first harvest cycle from a new planting. After this plant cane crop is harvested, meaning the stalks are cut down close to ground level, the root system and the base of the stalks, called stubble, remain alive in the soil, and if managed properly, this stubble will sprout new shoots on its own without any need to replant, producing a second crop known as the first ratoon. This ratoon crop can then itself be harvested and allowed to regrow again into a second ratoon, and this process can sometimes be repeated for several cycles, known as ratoon after ratoon, before yields decline enough that the field is eventually plowed out and replanted with fresh setts to restart the cycle.
