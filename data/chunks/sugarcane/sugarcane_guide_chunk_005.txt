Key factors include: - Maturity group (early, mid, or late), influencing the timing of peak sucrose accumulation and helping mills stagger the crushing season across varieties. - Cane yield potential and sugar recovery percentage under local conditions. - Disease resistance, particularly to red rot, smut, and other locally prevalent diseases, since sugarcane's long duration and vegetative propagation make disease management especially important - infected setts can carry disease directly into the next crop. - Pest resistance/tolerance, particularly to borers, which are among the most damaging sugarcane pests in many regions. - Ratooning ability, since a variety's capacity to regrow vigorously and productively from stubble after the plant crop is harvested directly affects the economics of multi-year ratoon cropping. - Tolerance to lodging, drought, or waterlogging as relevant to local conditions. Sugarcane is propagated using setts (stem cuttings), each containing one or more buds/nodes, rather than true seed, in virtually all commercial planting; farmers should use healthy, disease-free setts taken from a good-quality seed crop (ideally a dedicated, disease-free "seed nursery" crop), since using setts from an old, disease-affected, or poor-quality crop directly propagates diseases such as red rot and smut into the new planting. Setts are typically taken from the upper to middle portion of healthy, disease-free, actively growing stalks aged around 8-10 months, avoiding overly old, dried, or diseased cane, and are commonly treated with hot water and/or a fungicide before planting to reduce the carryover of sett-borne diseases such as smut and red rot. PLANTING TIME Sugarcane planting time varies considerably by region and is generally categorized into distinct planting seasons (such as spring and autumn planting in subtropical regions with a defined winter, or planting tied to the onset of favorable rains in more tropical regions).
