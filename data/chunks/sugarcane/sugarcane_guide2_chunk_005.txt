Cane yield, meaning the total tonnage of harvestable stalks produced per unit area, is one major factor, but just as important is sucrose content, often expressed as a commercial cane sugar percentage or CCS, since two varieties producing the same tonnage of cane can differ substantially in how much actual sugar can be extracted from that cane. Maturity duration also matters, with early maturing varieties ripening in a shorter window and late maturing varieties needing a longer season to reach peak sugar content, and farmers often plant a mix of early, mid, and late maturing varieties to spread out their harvest and keep the sugar mill supplied over a longer crushing season rather than having the entire crop ready at once. Ratooning ability, meaning how well a variety regrows and performs after being cut for a first harvest, is another major consideration, since varieties with strong ratooning ability allow farmers to get several profitable harvests from a single planting before yields decline enough to require replanting. Disease resistance, particularly resistance to major diseases like red rot and smut discussed further below, along with tolerance to lodging, drought, and cold, are all also factored into variety selection depending on local growing conditions. Land preparation for sugarcane typically involves deep plowing to break up compacted soil and allow good root penetration, since the crop will occupy the field for a long period and benefits from a deep, well-loosened root zone, followed by harrowing to break clods and level the field.
