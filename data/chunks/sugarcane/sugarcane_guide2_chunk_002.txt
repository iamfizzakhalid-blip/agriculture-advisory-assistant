Sugarcane does produce a flower, called an arrow or tassel, at the top of mature canes under certain daylength and climate conditions, but commercial sugarcane is not grown from the true seed produced by this flower, because seed-grown plants are highly variable and slow to establish; instead, sugarcane is propagated vegetatively from stem cuttings. The life cycle of a single sugarcane crop cycle, called plant cane, generally spans somewhere between ten and eighteen months depending on the variety and climate, which is considerably longer than the growing season of annual crops like maize, wheat, or rice. This long cycle is generally divided into four broad phases. The germination phase covers the first several weeks after planting, during which buds on the planted stem cuttings sprout and send up the first shoots, and this stage is quite sensitive to soil moisture and temperature, since the buds need adequate warmth and moisture to sprout reliably. The tillering phase follows, during which the plant sends up multiple additional shoots from its base, much like tillering in rice, and the number of productive tillers that survive to become harvestable stalks strongly influences the eventual yield of cane per unit area. After tillering slows down, the crop enters the grand growth phase, sometimes called the stalk elongation phase, which is the longest phase of the cycle and the period during which the plant grows most rapidly in height and thickness, building up the bulk of the biomass that will later store sugar; this phase demands the most water and nutrients of any stage in the crop's life.
