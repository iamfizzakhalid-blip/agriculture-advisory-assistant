After the plant crop is harvested, the stubble is generally managed through prompt trash/residue management, timely early irrigation and fertilizer application to stimulate vigorous ratoon sprouting, and early weed control, since ratoon crops can initially be more vulnerable to weed competition and slower establishment than a fresh plant crop. Ratoon yields often decline somewhat compared to the original plant crop, and this decline typically becomes more pronounced with successive ratoon cycles, influenced by factors such as accumulating disease and pest pressure in the stubble, gradual soil fertility depletion if not adequately replenished, and gradual decline in stand/tiller population over successive ratoons; good ratoon management (prompt post-harvest care, adequate and timely fertilization, disease and pest control, and gap-filling of missing stools where feasible) can significantly extend the number of economically viable ratoon crops taken from a single planting before the field needs to be replanted. The number of ratoon crops taken before replanting varies considerably by region, variety, management level, and economics, with some systems taking just one or two ratoons and others extending to several ratoon cycles under good management. CROP ROTATION AND CROPPING SYSTEMS Because sugarcane occupies the field for such an extended period, both as a plant crop and across subsequent ratoon cycles, crop rotation decisions are made less frequently than for annual crops but remain important for long-term soil health and pest/disease management.
