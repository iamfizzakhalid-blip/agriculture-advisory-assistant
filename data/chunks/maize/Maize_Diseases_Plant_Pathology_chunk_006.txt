The vesicle ruptures at the apical end liberating uniflagellated zoospores measuring 300 zoospores, which are smaller than the zoospores produced in resting sporangia. When these zoospores come in contact with a moist leaf, they move about for sometime with the help of their flagella and then come to rest. They germinate and infect the host cell. Sometimes they give rise to irregular structures which remain anchored to the host tissue by means of branched rhizoidal mycelium. These structures are thin-walled sporangia. Each such sporangium discharges at maturity, through an apical papilla, about 300 zoospores, which are smaller than the zoospores produced in resting sporangia. It is thought that they act as gametes. The mycelium in the host cell consists of fine fibrous coenocytic hyphae which connect large vegetative cells known as sammelzellen. These structures may eventually develop into resting sporangia and send out fibrous hyphae which produce terminal sporangia. The fungus is intracellular in the host tissue, and the fibrous hyphae grow from cell to cell. Nature and Recurrence of Disease (Disease Cycle) The fungus is an obligate parasite. The disease is soil-borne. The resting sporangia formed in the plant tissue remain viable in soil and in leaf sheath at least for 3 years. On maturity of the lesions they are released from the host tissue and are disseminated by wind. The resting sporangia require relatively high temperature (23°C to 30°C) for germination. Sanitation: the practices of field sanitation have been proved effective in this disease. Crop rotation: since the pathogen survives in the soil, crop rotation should be practiced. Resistant varieties: the resistant varieties should be grown.
