Hybrid maize is created by crossing two or more specifically bred parent lines, and the resulting seed shows what is called hybrid vigor, meaning noticeably higher yield, more uniform plants, and often better tolerance to stress. However, if a farmer saves seed from a hybrid crop and replants it the following year, that second generation will not breed true, and yields and uniformity will drop noticeably, which is why farmers growing hybrids are generally advised to purchase fresh seed each season rather than saving their own. In some countries, genetically modified maize varieties are also approved for use, most commonly engineered for resistance to certain insect pests or for tolerance to specific herbicides, though the legality and availability of these varieties varies a great deal from country to country. Preparing the land properly before sowing makes a real difference to how well maize establishes. This typically involves deep plowing every couple of years to break up compacted soil layers, followed by one or two rounds of harrowing to create a fine, crumbly seedbed that allows for even seed depth and good germination. Leveling the field helps water move evenly across it during irrigation and also helps prevent water from pooling in low spots. In areas with heavy rainfall or flooding risk, farmers often form ridges and furrows or raised beds to keep the root zone from becoming waterlogged. Many farmers also work farmyard manure or compost into the soil before sowing, which builds up organic matter and improves the soil's ability to hold both water and nutrients over time.
