Lodging, where stalks fall over, can result from weak stalks due to disease or potassium deficiency, from strong winds, from planting the crop too densely, or from applying a lot of nitrogen without enough potassium to balance it, since potassium is what gives the stalk much of its structural strength. When the crop is ready, harvesting decisions depend on what the maize is being grown for. Grain maize destined for storage and sale is usually harvested once it reaches about twenty to twenty five percent moisture and is then dried further, either naturally in the field or with mechanical drying, down to around twelve to fourteen percent moisture, which is considered safe for storage without significant risk of mold growth. Maize intended for silage, which is fermented whole plant fodder for livestock, is harvested earlier, generally somewhere between the dough and early dent stages, when the whole plant has the right balance of moisture and sugar content for good fermentation. Sweet corn, as mentioned earlier, is harvested much earlier still, at the milk stage, while the kernels are tender and sweet and before they begin drying down and turning starchy. Harvesting itself can be done by hand, picking ears individually, or with a mechanical combine harvester fitted with a corn head, which both picks and shells the grain in one pass. After harvest, proper drying down to around twelve to fourteen percent moisture before storage is absolutely critical, since grain that goes into storage too wet is highly prone to mold growth and the associated risk of mycotoxin contamination discussed earlier.
