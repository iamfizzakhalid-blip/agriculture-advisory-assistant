Weeds are a major concern in maize, particularly during the first thirty to forty five days after sowing, which is often referred to as the critical weed free period, because young maize plants compete very poorly against weeds for light, water, and nutrients during this window. Once the maize canopy closes and begins shading the ground, it becomes much more capable of suppressing weeds on its own. Farmers control weeds using a combination of approaches, including pre-emergence herbicides applied right after sowing before weeds have a chance to germinate, post-emergence herbicides applied later that are selective enough to kill young weeds without harming the maize itself, mechanical methods like inter-row cultivation or hand hoeing, and sometimes mulching to physically suppress weed growth. The specific weeds present and the herbicides available vary a great deal by region, so herbicide choice needs to match both the weeds actually present in the field and the safety profile for maize itself. On the pest side, one of the most significant threats to maize in recent years has been fall armyworm, a caterpillar pest that spread from the Americas into Africa and Asia and can cause severe damage if not caught early; its larvae feed inside the whorl of young plants, leaving behind a characteristic ragged, window pane pattern of holes in the leaves, and management usually relies on regular scouting to catch infestations early, targeted insecticide use, encouraging natural predators, or planting resistant Bt hybrids where they are available and legal.
