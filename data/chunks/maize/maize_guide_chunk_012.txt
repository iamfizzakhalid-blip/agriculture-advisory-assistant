Stalk rots, caused by various fungi or bacteria, weaken the stalk from the inside, making the plant more prone to lodging and reducing how well it can fill grain, and these problems tend to be worse when the plant is also dealing with a nutrient imbalance, especially low potassium, or drought stress. Ear rots and cob rots are a particular concern because, beyond just spoiling the grain, some of the fungi responsible can produce toxic compounds known as mycotoxins, including aflatoxin and fumonisin, which are genuinely dangerous to the health of both humans and livestock if contaminated grain ends up being consumed, and this risk is especially high when grain is stored in warm, humid conditions without proper drying. Downy mildew is a serious problem in some tropical growing regions, causing stunted growth and a streaked, pale pattern on the leaves. Various viral diseases, usually spread by insects like leafhoppers or aphids, can also cause stunting and a mottled, mosaic pattern in the leaves, and since these are hard to treat directly once a plant is infected, management focuses mainly on controlling the insect vectors and choosing resistant varieties. Beyond diseases and pests, maize can also suffer from a range of problems that are not caused by any living organism but instead come from stress or imbalance. Barren stalks, meaning plants that produce no ear at all, are usually the result of overcrowding, severe stress right around flowering time, or a serious nitrogen shortage. Poor kernel set, sometimes described as tip back because the missing kernels are concentrated near the tip of the ear, typically results from drought or heat stress during silking, which prevents the later-emerging silks near the tip from being pollinated in time.
