Brown Spot of Maize The pathogen is Physoderma zeae-maydis Shaw, class Chytridiomycetes, order Chytridiales, family Cladochytriaceae. This disease is commonly found in all the maize-growing areas of the world. It occurs throughout the south-eastern quarter of the United States. This was found in India in 1910 and in Illinois in 1911. However, in India this disease is not widely spread and is of minor importance. The symptoms of the disease occur on leaf blades, sheaths, culms, and rarely on the husks of the ears. The lower portion of the plant is usually more severely affected. The early symptom is the appearance of slightly bleached or yellowish spots which turn reddish-brown and finally brown. Such spots seldom exceed 1 mm in diameter and the blade and 5 mm on the midrib or sheath. The affected plant parts become rusty in appearance. Spots on the infected leaf blades die prematurely. The sheath becomes reddened and severely affected leaf blades are nearly rusty in appearance. Spots on the infected culms are generally situated near the nodes, and thus the tissues are weakened and the stem lodging occurs, causing heavy damage. The brown spot of maize is caused by Physoderma zeae-maydis Shaw. The brown spots on diseased plants contain smooth, brown, thick-walled resting sporangia measuring 18 to 24 microns by 20 to 30 microns. They are flattened at one side where a definite lid or cap is seen. On germination the lid of sporangium opens, and a thin vesicle comes out through the opening. The vesicle ruptures at the apical end liberating uniflagellated zoospores measuring 300 zoospores, which are smaller than the zoospores produced in resting sporangia.
