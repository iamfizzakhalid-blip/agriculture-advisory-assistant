After harvest, proper drying down to around twelve to fourteen percent moisture before storage is absolutely critical, since grain that goes into storage too wet is highly prone to mold growth and the associated risk of mycotoxin contamination discussed earlier. Stored grain should be kept in clean, dry, pest proof bins or containers, and it is generally best practice to avoid mixing newly harvested grain with older stored grain. Regular monitoring for storage pests like weevils and grain borers is also important, and appropriate storage protectants can be used where infestations are a recurring problem. Shelling, which means removing the kernels from the cob, is usually done once the grain has dried sufficiently, and can be accomplished by hand, with a simple hand-cranked sheller, or with a mechanical sheller for larger quantities. Maize is used in an enormous number of ways once it leaves the field. As human food, it is eaten as whole grain, ground into various types of flour or meal used in breads, porridges, and traditional dishes around the world, processed into breakfast cereals, and, in the case of sweet corn, eaten fresh as a vegetable. Globally, one of the single largest uses of maize is as animal feed, both as dried grain and as silage, for cattle, poultry, and pigs. Industrially, maize is processed into starch, into corn syrup and other sweeteners, and into ethanol for use as a biofuel, and the germ of the kernel can also be pressed for corn oil. The whole plant, whether used fresh or preserved as silage, also serves directly as fodder for livestock.
