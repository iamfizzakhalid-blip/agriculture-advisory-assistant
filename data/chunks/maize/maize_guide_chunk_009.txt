Other nutrients like sulfur, magnesium, and boron can also become limiting in certain soils, though less commonly than the ones already mentioned, and continuing to add organic matter through farmyard manure or compost helps improve how efficiently the crop uses whatever fertilizer is applied. Water management is just as important as nutrient management. If rainfall is not reliable, irrigation becomes especially critical at a few key points in the crop's life: during germination and establishment, around the knee high vegetative stage, during tasseling and silking, and during grain filling afterward. Of all of these, the two week window right around silking is by far the most sensitive, and allowing the crop to run short of water during this narrow period can cause yield losses far greater than the same level of water stress would cause at almost any other stage. At the same time, farmers need to avoid the opposite problem of waterlogging, since maize cannot tolerate standing water for more than a day or two, particularly during germination and the early vegetative stages, without suffering significant root damage and stand loss. Depending on the region and the resources available, irrigation might be delivered through furrows, sprinklers, drip systems, which are becoming more common because they use water efficiently, or the crop may simply be grown as a rainfed crop relying entirely on natural rainfall.
