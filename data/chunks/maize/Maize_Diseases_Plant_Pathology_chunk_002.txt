Conidia are olivaceous-brown, slightly curved, tapering towards the rounded ends, and vary in size and septation; usually they are 3 to 7 times transversely septate. Each conidium measures 80 to 120 microns by 20 to 24 microns in diameter. Crop rotation of susceptible grain crops with legumes or other nonsusceptible hosts reduces the overwintering inoculum. Head Smut of Maize The pathogen is Sphacelotheca reiliana (Kuhn) Clinton, synonym Ustilago reiliana Kuhn, class Basidiomycetes, order Ustilaginales, family Ustilaginaceae. In India, this disease occurs chiefly in Kashmir, Punjab, and Maharashtra. It has been known from India under the name Ustilago pulveracea Cke. It is known in the United States, Southern Europe, Africa, Australia, and Japan. Head smut of maize is usually first noticed on the tassel, sometimes converting the whole upper part to a mass of smut, sometimes affecting single flowers or a portion of the inflorescence only. Sometimes, sori are formed in individual flowers; in such cases, the flowers are much enlarged and deformed. Usually, when the tassel shows the smut, all the cobs below are destroyed or partially attacked; contrary to it, the tassel may escape where the cobs are all involved. In the cob, the sorus usually takes the form of a single large smut-mass enclosed when young with a smooth or lobed membrane, which encloses the whole part that should have borne grain. The smutted plants are often stunted in growth, brittle and remain green longer than the rest of the crop. The head smut of maize is caused by Sphacelotheca reiliana (Kuhn) Clinton.
