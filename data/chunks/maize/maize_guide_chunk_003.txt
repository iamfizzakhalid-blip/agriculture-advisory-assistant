After successful pollination, the kernels pass through a blister stage where they are small and filled with watery fluid, then a milk stage where the inside of the kernel looks milky white, then a dough stage where that milky fluid thickens into a paste, and then a dent stage, named for the small dent that forms at the crown of each kernel as it begins to dry and fill with starch. Eventually the crop reaches what is called physiological maturity, sometimes described by farmers as the black layer stage, because a small blackish layer forms where the kernel attaches to the cob. At this point the kernel has reached its maximum dry weight and stopped accumulating any more yield, even though the grain still contains a fairly high amount of moisture, often around thirty to thirty five percent, and everything that happens after this point, whether in the field or after harvest, is really just about drying the grain down rather than adding any more yield to it. When it comes to climate, maize grows best when average temperatures sit somewhere between about twenty one and thirty degrees Celsius. Seeds germinate poorly if soil temperatures drop below about ten degrees Celsius, and on the other end, temperatures above about thirty five degrees Celsius, especially if they coincide with dry conditions during flowering, can seriously damage pollination and yield.
