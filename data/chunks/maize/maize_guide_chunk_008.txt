A total nitrogen application of around one hundred twenty to one hundred fifty kilograms per hectare is common for well managed hybrid maize, and rather than applying it all at once, it is usually split into two or three doses, typically some at sowing, some around the time the plant is knee high, and some just before tasseling, both to match the plant's actual uptake pattern over time and to reduce the amount of nitrogen that gets lost to leaching. A farmer can often recognize nitrogen deficiency by yellowing that starts at the tip of the older, lower leaves and moves inward along the midrib in a distinctive V shape. Phosphorus is especially important early in the plant's life for root development, and because it does not move around easily in the soil, it is usually applied entirely at the time of sowing rather than split into later doses; a phosphorus deficient plant often shows a purplish discoloration on its leaves, especially in cold soil conditions. Potassium is important for keeping the stalk strong and helping the plant resist disease and fill grain properly, and a shortage of potassium typically shows up as yellowing or scorching along the margins of the older leaves. Zinc deficiency is also extremely common in maize growing regions and is easy to recognize because it produces broad pale or whitish bands running along the midrib of young leaves, something farmers sometimes call white bud, and this is usually corrected by applying zinc sulfate.
