The maize plant passes through a fairly well defined life cycle that is often described in growth stages. In the early part of its life, called the vegetative stage, the plant is simply putting out leaves and building its root system and stalk strength, and this stage is often tracked by counting how many leaves have fully emerged. During this vegetative period, the plant is also quietly determining how many rows of kernels its future ear will have, so stress during this window, though generally less damaging than stress later on, can still reduce the plant's potential. Eventually the plant reaches tasseling, when the male tassel emerges fully at the top, and shortly after that comes silking, when the silks emerge from the ears and pollination takes place, usually over about a week. This silking period is widely considered the single most critical stage in the entire life of the crop, because if the weather is extremely hot or dry at this exact time, pollen can lose viability or the timing between pollen shedding and silk emergence can fall out of sync, and either problem directly reduces the number of kernels that form, which means fewer kernels means lower yield no matter how well the rest of the season goes.
