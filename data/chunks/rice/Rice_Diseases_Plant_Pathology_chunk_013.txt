The pathogen is worldwide in distribution and also parasitizes other graminaceous hosts such as sorghum, maize, and sugarcane. The disease mainly affects the host during the seedling stage; symptoms are clearly seen in the nursery. Sometimes the grains fail to germinate or the seedlings fail to emerge above the soil. The most conspicuous and detectable symptoms appear from the sixth day after sowing in wet nurseries and continue up to the sixth week. The affected seedlings become thin, pale, and tall; such symptoms appear from the sixth day after sowing in wet nurseries and continue up to the sixth week. As soon as symptoms appear, seedlings wilt and die within 3 to 6 days. Mostly this is a nursery disease, and casualties may occur throughout the life of the crop. In the seventh or eighth week, it is noted that older diseased plants have produced fewer tillers than healthy plants. In severe infection, either the panicle does not develop, or a whitish, sterile ear is developed. The infected plants may be conspicuously above the general level of the crop. Another conspicuous symptom is the development of adventitious roots from the lower nodes of the culm, above the ground level. Such adventitious roots may be partially or completely enclosed by the leaf sheath. When we split infected culms, brown discolouration of the nodal tissues is seen. In later stages the mycelial mats and spores fill up the hollow internodes of infected plants; in later stages the mycelium appears outside the basal nodal zones, resulting in the formation of a pinkish bloom on the surface.
