Grain discoloration, sometimes linked to fungal infection during grain filling, can also reduce both the yield and the market quality of the harvested rice. In terms of harvesting, rice is generally harvested when the grains have reached physiological maturity, which farmers commonly judge by the color of the crop, watching for the majority of the panicles and the straw beneath them to turn from green to a golden yellow color, and by grain moisture, with harvest typically targeted somewhere around twenty to twenty five percent grain moisture, similar to the target range for maize. Harvesting too early results in a higher proportion of unfilled or chalky grains and lower overall yield, while harvesting too late increases losses from grains shattering out of the panicle and falling to the ground, as well as increasing vulnerability to lodging, birds, and pests. Harvesting can be done by hand with sickles, which remains common in many smallholder systems, particularly where field conditions are difficult for machinery, or by mechanical combine harvester, which cuts, threshes, and cleans the grain in a single pass and is increasingly common in larger, more mechanized rice growing regions. After harvest, freshly harvested rice, much like maize, needs to be dried down promptly, typically to around twelve to fourteen percent moisture, to prevent mold growth, insect infestation, and quality deterioration during storage; this drying can be done by spreading the grain out in the sun on a hard, clean surface, which is common in many smallholder systems, or through mechanical dryers in more industrialized operations.
