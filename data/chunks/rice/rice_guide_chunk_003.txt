Rice grows best with average temperatures somewhere between about twenty and thirty five degrees Celsius, and it is generally more heat tolerant than many other cereal crops, though very high temperatures during flowering, above around thirty five degrees Celsius, can cause sterility and poor grain set in the same way that heat stress damages maize during silking. Cold temperatures, particularly below about twenty degrees Celsius during flowering, can also cause spikelet sterility in many varieties, which is a significant concern in higher altitude or higher latitude rice growing regions. Because so much rice is grown under flooded conditions, water requirement is unusually high compared to most other cereals, with irrigated lowland rice commonly needing somewhere in the range of one thousand to one thousand five hundred millimeters of water or more across the season when field flooding is included, though this varies enormously depending on soil type, climate, and the specific water management system used. Rice does need good sunlight for strong yields, but it is somewhat more shade tolerant during parts of its growth than maize is. Soil and water management is really where rice diverges most sharply from most other major crops, because the majority of the world's rice is grown as lowland or paddy rice, meaning the field is deliberately flooded with a shallow layer of standing water for most of the growing season. This standing water suppresses many weeds, moderates soil temperature, and supplies the plant with water reliably, and rice has special internal air channels called aerenchyma that allow oxygen to move down from the leaves to the roots even when the roots are sitting in waterlogged, oxygen poor soil, which is exactly the kind of condition that would kill most other crops, including maize, within a day or two.
