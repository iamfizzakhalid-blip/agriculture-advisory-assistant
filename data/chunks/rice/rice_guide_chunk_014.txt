From a farming and economic standpoint, the major cost inputs in rice production are seed, particularly for farmers using hybrid varieties who need to purchase new seed each season, fertilizer, the labor and water costs associated with land preparation, transplanting, and irrigation, which tend to be higher in rice than in most dryland cereals precisely because of the flooding and, where used, transplanting steps, and crop protection inputs like pesticides and herbicides. Yield potential varies widely with management, water availability, and variety, with rainfed or upland systems with minimal inputs often producing somewhere around one and a half to three tons per hectare, while well managed irrigated systems using modern high yielding or hybrid varieties can commonly exceed six to eight tons per hectare and sometimes considerably more under intensive management. Because rice fields are so often flooded for extended periods, continuous rice cultivation can also lead to certain soil and pest management challenges over time, so many farming systems rotate rice with a dryland crop, such as wheat in parts of South Asia or various legumes and vegetables elsewhere, both to break pest and disease cycles that build up under continuous flooding and to make more diverse use of the land and water available across the year.
