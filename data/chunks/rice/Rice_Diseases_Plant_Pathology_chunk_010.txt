In Tamil Nadu, in years of heavy infection, yield loss goes up to 75 percent; in Punjab, the crop is sometimes subjected to heavy infestation in August-September with yield loss ranging from 75 to 100 percent of the crop. The disease is commonly found in the transplanted crop from July to September. Small, dark lesions first develop on the outer leaf sheaths at the water line. These lesions gradually make their appearance on the inner leaf sheaths and culm; the affected tissues become rotten, and numerous small dark brown sclerotia form in the tissues of the leaf sheath. In later stages, mycelial mats and sclerotia are copiously formed inside the culm also, though rotting remains restricted to a few lower internodes. As plants mature, infection intensity increases, and the culms collapse, causing the plants to lodge heavily. Due to the stimulation caused by the fungus, tillering is increased and plants remain green until grain formation. When infection takes place later in the growing season, the grains are much shrivelled. When one plant in a clump shows the characteristic late shoots at the base, all other plants in that clump are similarly attacked, and every ear has a considerable number, often 50 percent, of light grains. The hyphae of Sclerotium oryzae penetrate the cells and large air spaces among the main vascular bundles of the culm and leaf sheath. The mycelium is greyish-white to pale-olive in colour and forms numerous small irregular appresoria; the hyphae also enter the hollow stem and the space between culm and sheaths, forming a grey, pale-olive, woolly growth.
