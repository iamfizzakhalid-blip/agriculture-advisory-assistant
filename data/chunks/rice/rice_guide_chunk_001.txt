Rice, known scientifically as Oryza sativa for the most widely cultivated species, is a semi-aquatic annual grass and one of the most important staple food crops in the world, feeding more than half of the global population as a primary source of calories, especially across Asia. Unlike maize, rice is a C3 plant rather than a C4 plant, which means it photosynthesizes somewhat less efficiently in very hot conditions and has different water and nutrient dynamics, but its defining biological feature is its strong adaptation to growing in standing water, something very few other major food crops can tolerate. The plant has a fibrous root system, hollow jointed stems called culms, long narrow leaves with a small membranous structure called a ligule where the leaf blade meets the sheath, and it produces its grain in a branched flower cluster at the top of the stem called a panicle, which holds many individual spikelets, each of which can develop into a single grain of rice enclosed in a tough outer husk. Rice is primarily self-pollinating, meaning each flower typically fertilizes itself rather than depending on wind or insects to bring in pollen from another plant, which is a significant practical difference from maize, since it makes rice varieties much easier to keep pure from one season to the next and reduces the risk of unwanted cross-pollination between different varieties grown nearby. The life cycle of rice is generally divided into three broad phases: the vegetative phase, the reproductive phase, and the ripening phase.
