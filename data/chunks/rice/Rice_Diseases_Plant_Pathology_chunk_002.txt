The pathogen also invades the nodal tissue of the culms, causing the tissue to shrink and turn dark grey or almost black; the culms may break at the infected nodes, with blackening extending 1 to 2 cm. When the ear emerges, the neck of the panicle becomes infected, showing bluish lesions on the neck of the culm and on the branches near the base of the panicle; this phase is known as black neck or neck blast. The neck region becomes black and shrivelled, and if this occurs soon after the ears emerge, the grains remain chaffy, the panicle breaks at the neck, and the ear droops due to weakening of the neck tissue. Maximum damage is caused by necrosis in the neck region. Individual spikelets are also infected, developing small, circular brown lesions on the glume, lemma, and palea, and grain set in infected ears is completely to partially inhibited. The blast disease is caused by the imperfect fungus Pyricularia oryzae Cav.; its perfect stage is not yet known. The fungus, when young, has hyaline and septate mycelium; on maturity, its colour changes to olive-brown, and the mycelium may be inter- or intra-cellular within host tissues. The conidiophores emerge through the stomata or epidermal cells, singly or in small clusters, and are septate (two to four septa), greyish, slender, and rarely branched. Seven to nine conidia are produced terminally on each conidiophore, with a characteristic zig-zag terminal growth denoting sympodial growth. Each conidium is obpyriform or obclavate, hyaline, septate, and bears a small basal appendage, ranging from 14 to 40 microns in length and 6 to 15 microns in width.
