Land preparation for lowland rice differs noticeably from that of most dryland crops because it typically involves puddling, a process in which the field is flooded and then plowed or harrowed while wet, which breaks down soil structure into a smooth, compacted, nearly impermeable layer that helps the field hold standing water with minimal seepage and also helps suppress weeds by burying weed seeds and creating an oxygen poor environment they cannot easily germinate in. Before puddling, the field is usually plowed once while relatively dry to incorporate crop residue and any organic amendments like farmyard manure or compost, and the field is carefully leveled, since even, precise leveling is especially important in rice because uneven ground leads to some areas being too deeply flooded and others not flooded enough, both of which hurt yield. Small earthen bunds are built or maintained around the field edges to hold the irrigation water in place. For upland or aerobic rice systems, land preparation looks much more like preparation for other cereal crops, involving plowing and harrowing to create a good seedbed without any deliberate flooding or puddling step. Rice can be established in the field in a few different ways. Transplanting is the traditional and still very widely used method in much of Asia, in which rice seed is first sown densely in a separate nursery bed, allowed to grow into young seedlings over roughly three to four weeks, and then those seedlings are manually or mechanically pulled up and transplanted individually into the puddled, flooded main field at a spacing of roughly fifteen to twenty centimeters between plants, though this varies by variety and region; this method gives the crop a head start against weeds and allows more precise control
