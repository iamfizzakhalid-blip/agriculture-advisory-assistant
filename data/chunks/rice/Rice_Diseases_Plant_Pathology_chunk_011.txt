Sclerotia develop on the free mycelium lining the inner surface, and also in the air spaces; they are nearly spherical, smooth, black, shiny, and measure 200 to 350 microns in diameter, visible to the naked eye as black dots. The sclerotial stage of the fungus (Sclerotium oryzae) is commonly found on paddy and some other wild grasses. In the conidial stage (Helminthosporium sigmoideum), the conidiophores are dark, erect, nodular, 8 to 10 septate, branched, 100 to 150 microns in length and about 3 microns in width. The conidia are falcate, sigmoid, 3-septate, pale-olivaceous, and borne singly at the tip of the conidiophore, measuring 55 to 65 by 11 to 14 microns. The mycelium is branched, septate, inter- and intra-cellular within host tissue. The perfect stage (Leptosphaeria salvinii) may or may not be present. The perithecia are globose, black, clustered in the parenchyma of the leaf sheath, measuring 350 to 400 microns in diameter; each perithecium contains clavate asci about 120 microns long, each containing 8 ascospores. The ascospores are oblong, fusiform, 3-septate, slightly curved, and 60 to 90 microns long. This stage is less common and doubtfully occurs in India. The disease is soil-borne. Pathogenically, the sclerotial stage is most important. The sclerotia of the fungus survive in the soil for several months and carry over the disease from year to year; sometimes the sclerotia remain viable in the stubbles for two years. The sclerotia float on water and spread from field to field or plant to plant.
