The vegetative phase begins with germination and seedling establishment and continues through active tillering, which is the process by which the young rice plant sends up multiple additional stems, called tillers, from its base, and the number of productive tillers a plant produces has a major influence on how many panicles, and therefore how much grain, the plant will ultimately produce. This phase ends around what is called panicle initiation, the point at which the growing point inside the stem switches from producing more leaves to beginning to form the young panicle. The reproductive phase then covers panicle development, the emergence of the panicle from the leaf sheath known as booting and heading, and flowering, during which the tiny flowers open briefly and self-pollinate; this flowering stage, much like silking in maize, is highly sensitive to stress, and extreme heat, cold, or drought at this exact point can cause a high percentage of spikelets to fail to set grain, directly reducing yield. After flowering, the plant enters the ripening phase, during which the fertilized grains fill with starch, passing through a watery stage, then a milky stage, then a dough stage, before finally reaching physiological maturity, at which point the grains have hardened and the plant's leaves and stems typically begin to turn golden yellow, which is the visual cue farmers use to judge that the crop is nearing harvest time. Climate requirements for rice center around warmth and, for most varieties, an ample and reliable water supply.
