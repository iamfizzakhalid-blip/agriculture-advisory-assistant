In transplanted systems, the head start given to rice seedlings over weeds, combined with standing water, generally keeps weed pressure more manageable than in direct seeded systems, where weeds can germinate and grow alongside the rice from the very start, making direct seeded rice fields generally more dependent on herbicides or manual weeding to stay clean. Both pre-emergence and post-emergence herbicides are used in rice, chosen to match local weed populations and applied carefully to avoid crop injury, and manual or mechanical weeding, particularly during the first several weeks after establishment, remains common practice in many smallholder systems. A number of pests affect rice production. The rice stem borer, whose larvae tunnel into the stem, is one of the most damaging pests worldwide, causing a symptom called dead heart in young plants, where the central shoot dies, and a symptom called whitehead in older plants, where the developing panicle turns white and produces no grain because its vascular connection to the rest of the plant has been severed. The brown planthopper is another major pest, a small sap sucking insect that can build up to enormous populations very quickly under the right conditions and cause a distinctive symptom known as hopperburn, where large patches of the field suddenly turn brown and die as if scorched, and it can also transmit certain viral diseases. Rice leaf folder larvae fold and roll rice leaves and feed inside them, reducing the leaf area available for photosynthesis. Gall midge causes an abnormal tubular growth on the plant called a silver shoot, which produces no grain. Rice bugs feed directly on developing grains, causing discolored or empty kernels.
