The sclerotia are responsible for primary infection, and when conidia are produced, they are also spread through irrigation water, causing and intensifying secondary infection. Excessive flooding favours the disease. In South India, disease intensity increases in swampy places, and waterlogging also leads to increased infection. Early-sown crops and early-maturing varieties are infected to a larger extent. Excess nitrogenous fertilizers increase disease infestation, and the effects of phosphorus also increase infection intensity. However, potash application along with phosphatic and nitrogenous fertilizers increases the crop's resistance against the disease. The best method of disease control is the use of resistant varieties. Clean cultivation is recommended: since the inoculum survives on the stubbles, burning the stubbles of affected crops has been suggested, and irrigation water flow should be avoided from affected fields to healthy ones. Crop rotation has been proved beneficial to some extent and may be practiced in regions where other crops, like sugarcane or banana, take part in the rotation. Proper manuring, with adequate quantities of potassic fertilizers, has been proved quite useful in increasing yields and reducing the severity of the disease. Foot Rot of Rice The pathogen is Fusarium moniliforme Sheld., class Deuteromycetes, order Moniliales, family Tuberculariaceae. The foot rot of rice caused by Fusarium moniliforme was first reported from Italy in 1877 and was known as bakanae disease. From Japan, the disease was first described in 1898. The foot rot disease of rice seedlings has been recorded from South India. The pathogen is worldwide in distribution and also parasitizes other graminaceous hosts such as sorghum, maize, and sugarcane.
