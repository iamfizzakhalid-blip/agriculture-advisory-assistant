The mycelium of the fungus is both inter- and intra-cellular within the mesophyll tissue of the leaves. The conidiophores emerge as lateral branches from hyphae, usually in tufts through stomata. Each conidiophore is 44 to 600 microns long, 3 to 9 microns wide, septate, dark brown at the base, and possesses characteristic knee-bends. The conidiophores bear conidia singly and successively at regular intervals terminally in a sympodial manner; the points of attachment of successive conidia are denoted by scars at regular intervals on the conidiophores. Conidia are septate, olive-brown in colour, slightly curved, and tapering towards rounded ends. The conidia are generally 5 to 10 septate, about 14 to 1600 microns long and 10 to 26 microns broad. The conidia germinate by producing germ tubes mostly from the end-cells. The temperature at which the conidia form has considerable effect on their shape and colour; conidia formed at lower temperatures are darker and straighter. Environmental factors are largely responsible for the incidence and intensity of infection. Rapid spread and development of the disease is favoured by continuous rains with cloudy weather and higher day temperatures (28°C to 30°C), generally in the later part of the growing season, September to October. However, seedling infection occurred when infected seeds were sown and low temperature (18°C to 22°C) prevailed. The optimum temperature for leaf infection ranges from 22°C to 30°C. A relative humidity of over 92 percent favours disease incidence, and spores do not germinate readily below 89 percent relative humidity.
