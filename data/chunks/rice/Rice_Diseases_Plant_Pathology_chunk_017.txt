The mycelium may be seen in the stem of affected plants, running between cells of the vertical sub-epidermal row of parenchyma, under the stomata. Spores form in thin-walled terminal swellings on short branches; they are globose or rarely elliptical, light to dark brown when mature, measuring 20 to 24 microns in diameter, giving a peculiar impression of a dark band furnished with projecting curved spines. The structure of the walls of spore is a hyaline membrane, somewhat gelatinous when moist and giving the adhesive properties of the spores. Mixed with the spores are numerous large yellowish or sub-hyaline cells representing immature spores. Fragments of septate hyphae may also be seen between the spores. The spores germinate after a period of dormancy. When put in water, after 3 days they give out a promycelium, septate at the tip, bearing at its end a cluster of several needle-shaped sporidia, 38 to 53 microns in length, divided into 3 to 4 cells after falling from the promycelium. Sporidia do not fuse or form H-shaped structures. This disease is air-borne. Infection takes place when the ears have just emerged from the leaf sheaths. The sporidia are carried by convection currents to the flowers from germinating spores. The sporidia extend germ tubes into the young ovaries of the flowers where the fungus finally settles down outside the aleurone layer, or under the thin membrane which forms the skin of the grain, and consists of the wall of the ovary and outer layers of the true seed. Gradually an extensive cavity is formed between the seed covering and the endosperm, and the spore-mass may entirely replace the starch cells of the endosperm, or may form only a layer on the endosperm.
