Because of this reliance on flooding, lowland rice is generally grown on heavier soils with good water holding capacity, often clay or clay loam soils that can hold a standing water layer without excessive seepage, and fields are typically leveled very precisely and surrounded by small earthen bunds to keep irrigation water contained. Rice can also be grown without flooding, in what is called upland or aerobic rice, generally on well drained soils similar to those used for other cereals, relying on rainfall or limited irrigation rather than standing water, though yields in upland systems are usually lower than in well managed flooded systems since the crop does not have the same buffer against water stress. Ideal soil pH for rice is roughly five point five to seven, though rice is notably more tolerant of both acidic and waterlogged, low oxygen soil conditions than most other major crops, which is part of why it can be productively grown in low lying deltas and floodplains that would be unsuitable for crops like maize or wheat. There are several important classifications of rice varieties that matter to how it is grown and used. In terms of grain type, rice is often divided into long grain, medium grain, and short grain varieties, with long grain rice, such as basmati or jasmine types, generally cooking up light and separate, while short and medium grain varieties tend to be stickier when cooked, a property valued in many East Asian cuisines and in dishes like risotto or sushi.
