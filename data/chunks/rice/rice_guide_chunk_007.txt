Direct seeding, by contrast, involves sowing rice seed straight into the main field rather than through a nursery, either by broadcasting seed onto a flooded or saturated field, or by drilling seed into a drier, prepared seedbed similar to how other cereals are sown, and this method saves labor and water in some systems but often requires more intensive weed control since the crop does not get the same head start that transplanted seedlings have. Seed rate varies a great deal depending on the establishment method, with transplanted systems typically needing far less seed per unit area than direct seeded systems, since transplanting allows very efficient, deliberate spacing while direct seeding usually oversows to compensate for uneven germination. Nutrient management in rice follows broadly similar principles to maize but with some important differences related to the flooded growing environment. Nitrogen is again the most critical nutrient and the one most likely to limit yield, with typical total applications for high yielding irrigated rice often falling somewhere around one hundred to one hundred fifty kilograms per hectare, though this varies by variety, yield target, and region, and as with maize it is usually split into multiple doses over the season, commonly at or shortly after transplanting, at active tillering, and again around panicle initiation, both to match the plant's nutrient demand at each stage and because nitrogen can be lost quite readily from flooded rice soils through processes like ammonia volatilization and leaching,
