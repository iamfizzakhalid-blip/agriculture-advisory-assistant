A relative humidity of over 92 percent favours disease incidence, and spores do not germinate readily below 89 percent relative humidity. Both excess and deficiency of nitrogenous fertilization also favour disease incidence and development. Susceptibility of leaves to infection increases with the age of the plants. The disease is primarily seed-borne. The fungus is found as dormant mycelium in the husk and on the kernel, and it often infects the germinating grain. Secondary infection is caused by air-borne conidia, generally produced either from primary infected plants or from other sources. Sometimes conidia survive on straw and stubbles left in the field after harvest. Collateral hosts provide air-borne conidia for secondary infection; the pathogen infects collateral hosts such as Setaria italica, Pennisetum typhoides, Saccharum officinarum (sugarcane), Triticum aestivum (wheat), Zea mays (maize), and Eleusine coracana. Cultivation of resistant varieties offers a cheap, reliable, and easy method of control. Suggested resistant varieties include CH 13, CH 45, CH 20, T1 41, T 498-2A, T 2114, Bam 10, IET 13238, CR-84-30, J. B.83, JBS 1510, Nagra 41-14, and Dakar Nagra 273-32, and Kalma 219 for West Bengal, among others. Since the disease is seed-borne, treatment of seeds with organo-mercurials has been recommended for eradication of primary infection. It has been found that the best control is achieved by seed treatment with ceresan, with the seeds needing to be stored for four weeks after treatment before sowing. Seed treatment with agrosan GN has also given satisfactory results; since the pathogen survives inside the husk, chemical treatments may not be very effective.
