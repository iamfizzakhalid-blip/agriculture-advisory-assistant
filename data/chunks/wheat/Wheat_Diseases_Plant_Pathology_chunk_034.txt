The conidia are disseminated by outer agencies such as wind, water, insects, etc. On the approach of suitable moisture and temperature they germinate by producing one or more tubes. The distal end of the germ tube of conidium appresses the surface of the leaf and develops into a flat appressorium. A peg-like structure is given out from the appressorium and penetrates the epidermal cells of the host. The distal end of this peg-like structure swells up into vesicle which gives rise to finger-like branches called haustoria. The cleistothecia generally appear at about the same time when ears are developed. Each cleistothecium arises as the result of a sexual act consisting in the fusion of parts of the contents of two specialized cells — the antheridium (male) and ascogonium (female). The cleistothecia are round or sub-globose, bright reddish-brown, about 200 microns in diameter, mostly and partly immersed in the mycelium. During development, the wall of the ascocarp (cleistothecium) is differentiated into inner and outer layers. The inner layer consists of thin-walled hyaline cells rich in cytoplasm; it supplies the food material to the developing asci. The function of the outer layer is protective. Each cleistothecium contains 20-30 asci. The asci are cylindrical or ovate, 70-108 by 25-40 microns, and each ascus contains 8 (rarely 4) elliptical, single-celled, hyaline ascospores, measuring 20-23 by 10-13 microns. The ascospores are released with jerk from asci. The ascospores germinate readily in moist air, giving rise to one or more germ tubes.
