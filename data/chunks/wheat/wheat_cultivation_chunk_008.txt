Depending on soil type, rainfall, climate, and irrigation method, wheat generally requires about 4-6 irrigations under flood/furrow irrigation systems; sandy soils need more frequent, lighter irrigations, while heavier clay soils can be irrigated less frequently with larger amounts. Sprinkler and drip irrigation systems, where available, can improve water use efficiency considerably compared to flood irrigation, allowing more precise, frequent applications of smaller amounts of water and reducing water losses to runoff, deep percolation, and evaporation. Avoiding water stress during crown root initiation and grain filling stages has the greatest overall impact on final yield; over-irrigation, on the other hand, encourages lodging, waterlogging-related root diseases, and nutrient leaching, particularly nitrogen loss. FERTILIZER AND NUTRIENT MANAGEMENT Wheat requires a balanced supply of macronutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium) as well as secondary and micronutrients (sulfur, zinc, manganese, boron, among others) for optimal growth and yield. - Nitrogen (N): The most yield-limiting nutrient in most wheat production systems; it drives vegetative growth, tillering, leaf area, and ultimately grain protein content. It should be applied in split doses rather than all at once, to reduce losses through leaching, volatilization, and denitrification, and to better match crop demand across growth stages: typically a basal dose at sowing, with the remainder split between the crown root initiation stage and the tillering/jointing stage irrigations. Excess nitrogen without balancing potassium can cause excessive vegetative growth, delayed maturity, lodging, and increased disease susceptibility (especially rust and powdery mildew). - Phosphorus (P): Essential for early root development, tillering, and energy transfer within the plant; because phosphorus is relatively immobile in soil, it is usually applied fully as a basal dose at or before sowing,
