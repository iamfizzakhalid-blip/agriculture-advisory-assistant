Wheat can be grown on a wide range of soils but performs best on well-drained loamy or clay-loam soils with good water-holding capacity and good structure. Soil pH between 6.0 and 7.5 is considered ideal; strongly acidic or alkaline/saline soils reduce nutrient availability and yield. Waterlogged, poorly drained, or compacted soils are unsuitable and promote root diseases, poor tillering, and lodging. Deep soils with good organic matter content support stronger root systems and higher yield potential. LAND PREPARATION Proper land preparation ensures good seed-to-soil contact, adequate root development, and efficient water use. Typical steps include: 1. Deep plowing (once every 2-3 years or after a deep-rooted previous crop) to break hardpan, improve aeration, and bury crop residues and weed seeds. 2. Two to three subsequent harrowings or cultivations with a disc or tine cultivator to break clods, mix in manure, and create a fine, firm seedbed. 3. Planking/leveling the field, which is critical for uniform irrigation water distribution and to prevent water stagnation in low spots and drought stress in high spots. 4. Removing perennial weeds, stubble, and previous crop residues, or incorporating residues well in advance of sowing so they decompose rather than tying up soil nitrogen. 5. Applying farmyard manure (FYM), compost, or green manure before the last plowing to improve soil organic matter, structure, water-holding capacity, and microbial activity. 6. Where laser land leveling technology is available, it significantly improves irrigation efficiency, uniform crop stand, and can improve yield while reducing water use. WHEAT VARIETIES AND SEED SELECTION Wheat varieties differ in maturity duration, yield potential, disease resistance, lodging resistance, grain quality, and adaptation to local climate and soil.
