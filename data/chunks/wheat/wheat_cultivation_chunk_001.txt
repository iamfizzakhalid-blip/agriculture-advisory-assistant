Wheat Cultivation Complete Guide: Botany, Varieties, Sowing, Fertilizer, Irrigation, Diseases, Pests, Growth Stages, Harvesting, and Yield Improvement INTRODUCTION TO WHEAT Wheat (Triticum aestivum, common/bread wheat; Triticum durum, durum wheat) is one of the world's most important cereal crops and a staple food source for a large share of the global population. It belongs to the grass family Poaceae and the tribe Triticeae. Wheat grain is used to produce flour for bread, chapati, biscuits, pasta, and many other food products, as well as for animal feed and industrial starch production. Wheat straw is also used as animal fodder and bedding. Globally, wheat is grown on more land area than almost any other crop, and it is a rabi (winter) crop in South Asia, sown in autumn/winter and harvested in spring, while in temperate regions it may be grown as either winter wheat (autumn-sown) or spring wheat (spring-sown), depending on climate and variety type. BOTANICAL CHARACTERISTICS Wheat is a self-pollinated annual grass. Its root system is fibrous, with both seminal (seed) roots emerging at germination and adventitious (crown) roots developing later from the crown region near the soil surface. The crown roots become the dominant root system and are critical for water and nutrient uptake, which is why the crown root initiation stage is considered so important for irrigation and fertilizer timing. The stem is a hollow culm with several nodes, and the plant produces multiple tillers (side shoots) from the crown, each capable of producing a productive ear (spike) under good management. The inflorescence is a spike (ear) composed of spikelets, each containing florets that develop into grains after pollination.
