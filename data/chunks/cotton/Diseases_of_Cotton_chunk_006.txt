The plugging of vessels interferes with the flow of water, and wilting of leaves results. The fungus secretes toxic substances, which also cause the wilting of the plant. The mycelium is hyaline, and produces macro- and microconidia. The macroconidia are 1 to 5 septate, hyaline, thin-walled, linear, and falcate, whereas the microconidia are elliptical to spherical, thin-walled, and one or two-celled. The chlamydospores are formed only in culture media. This is a soil-borne as well as seed-borne disease. This fungus is a facultative parasite and can remain viable in the soil for a long time. The fungus enters the host root when the plants are 1 to 3 weeks old. The fungus multiplies and moves to the xylem vessels, and ultimately the vessels become plugged by an abundance of mycelium. The soil-borne fungal hyphae and chlamydospores play the major role in perpetuation of the disease. The soil temperature ranging from 20 to 30 degrees Celsius favours the disease incidence. Hot and dry periods followed by rains also favour the disease. Nitrogenous fertilizers increase the incidence. The disease is restricted to the black cotton soils, which are heavy clay with an alkaline reaction. The use of resistant varieties is the only appropriate method of controlling this disease. Resistant varieties which have the desired agronomic and commercial characters have been evolved and cultivated in different states of India. In India, all the New World Cottons (Gossypium hirsutum, G. barbadense) are immune to wilt, even those which are highly susceptible to the disease in the countries from which they have been imported, for example, the United States.
