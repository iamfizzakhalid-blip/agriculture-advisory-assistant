SQUARE AND BOLL SHEDDING Cotton plants naturally shed a significant proportion of the squares, flowers, and young bolls they initially set, a normal physiological process, but excessive shedding beyond the natural level reduces yield and can result from several stress factors including water stress (both drought and waterlogging), extreme temperatures (particularly prolonged high temperatures during flowering), nutrient imbalances (especially boron deficiency and excessive nitrogen leading to excessive vegetative competition), and pest damage (particularly sucking pests and bollworms feeding directly on squares and young bolls). Managing shedding involves maintaining balanced, timely irrigation and fertilization, addressing micronutrient deficiencies where present, and controlling relevant pests, since shedding is often a combined outcome of several interacting stress factors rather than a single cause. PICKING AND HARVEST Cotton bolls do not mature simultaneously across the plant or field, since flowering and boll set occur progressively over an extended period; as a result, picking is generally carried out in multiple rounds (commonly three or more) over several weeks to months, harvesting only bolls that have fully matured and opened at each round, rather than a single harvest event as with many grain crops. Hand picking, still widely used in many regions, allows selective picking of only mature, opened bolls and tends to produce cleaner lint with less trash contamination, but is labor-intensive. Mechanical picking (using spindle pickers or strippers) is faster and less labor-intensive but requires more uniform boll opening across the field, which is sometimes promoted through the use of a chemical defoliant/boll opener applied late in the season to remove leaves and encourage more even, complete boll opening before mechanical harvest.
