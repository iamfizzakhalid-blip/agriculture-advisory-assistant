In India, all the New World Cottons (Gossypium hirsutum, G. barbadense) are immune to wilt, even those which are highly susceptible to the disease in the countries from which they have been imported, for example, the United States. Wilt occurs in that country in sandy acidic soils, and when those American varieties are grown in the heavy clayey and alkaline soils of India, they acquire immunity to the disease. As a seed treatment, the seed-borne phase can be destroyed by treating the seeds with organomercurials to some extent. Regarding exposure of soil to sun, in north India, after deep ploughing, the soil is exposed to sun in June-July to kill the pathogen. Proper manuring is also important: potash application reduces wilt incidence, whereas nitrogenous fertilizers are responsible for increasing disease incidence. Angular Leaf-Spot, Blackarm and Boll Rot of Cotton (A Bacterial Disease) The pathogen causing this disease is Xanthomonas malvacearum (E. F. Smith) Dowson. In its systematic position, it belongs to Class Schizomycetes, Order Pseudomonadales, Family Pseudomonadaceae. Distribution and Background This disease occurs in all cotton-growing countries of the world. The disease is responsible for heavy losses and has been supposed to be the third major disease of cotton. It was first recorded in the United States of America by Smith in 1920, where it was known as angular leaf-spot, as the spotting of the leaves was the most obvious symptom. Smith also established the connection between the leaf spot, the stem infections known as blackarm, and the attack on the bolls. The stem infections, known as blackarm, cause more serious damage than the leaf-spot. On the leaves, small spots appear, angular in shape, as the primary infections are limited by the small veins.
