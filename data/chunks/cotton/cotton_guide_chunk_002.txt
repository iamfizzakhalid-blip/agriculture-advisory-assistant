After fertilization, the flower develops into a boll (seed capsule) containing several locules, each with multiple seeds; the seed surface is covered with the two types of hairs that make up cotton fiber - long fibers (lint), which are spun into yarn, and short fibers (fuzz/linters), which remain attached to the seed and are used in industrial products such as cellulose derivatives. As the boll matures and dries, it splits open (dehisces) and the fluffy white or cream-colored lint becomes visible and ready for picking. TYPES AND CLASSIFICATION OF COTTON - Upland cotton (Gossypium hirsutum): The dominant commercial type grown worldwide, accounting for the vast majority of global cotton production; medium to long staple fiber suitable for most textile uses. - Desi/Asiatic cotton (Gossypium arboreum and Gossypium herbaceum): Traditional types grown mainly in parts of South Asia, generally more hardy and tolerant to adverse conditions but producing coarser, shorter fiber and lower yields than upland cotton. - Extra-long staple/Egyptian and Pima cotton (Gossypium barbadense): Produces very long, fine, high-value fiber used for premium textiles, but generally requires more specific climatic conditions and management. - Bt cotton: Genetically modified varieties incorporating genes from the soil bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis, which confer resistance to certain bollworm pests, reducing the need for insecticide sprays against these specific pests; widely adopted in many cotton-growing countries, though effectiveness against non-target pests (such as sucking insects) is limited, and resistance management practices are needed to maintain efficacy over time. CLIMATE AND SOIL REQUIREMENTS Cotton is a warm-season crop requiring a long, frost-free growing period with plenty of sunshine.
