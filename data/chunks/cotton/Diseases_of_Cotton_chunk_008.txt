On the leaves, small spots appear, angular in shape, as the primary infections are limited by the small veins. The spots are dark brown in colour, and they may coalesce to form large blotches on newly formed leaves; the attack is limited to the tissue on either side of the main veins. In wet weather, the narrow dark-brown areas extend up to the petioles. However, in dry weather conditions, the severely attacked leaves fall off, and the plants remain barren. Elongated and discoloured areas appear on the young shoots and older branches. The stem infections are sunken and turn black, from which symptom the name blackarm has been derived; such symptoms may extend many inches in length. Affected leaves show lesions on the leaf blade, and similar lesions can be seen on the stems and seedlings. The bolls may be attacked at all stages of development. The young bolls may be killed and fall off, whereas the older bolls bear large dark spots which turn black. The seeds also become contaminated, and the lint is often destroyed or rendered useless. The angular leaf-spot or blackarm disease of cotton is caused by a bacterium, Xanthomonas malvacearum (E. F. Smith) Dowson. The bacterium is a short motile rod, provided with a single polar flagellum. It is gram-negative, and measures 1.0 to 1.2 by 0.7 to 0.9 microns. The bacterium does not enter the vascular bundles but is confined to the parenchyma. From the leaf-stem lesions, the bacteria are disseminated by wind-blown rain onto the bolls, where spots are formed. In the infected capsules, the bacteria penetrate to the developing seed and form slimy masses around the hairs surrounding the micropyle.
