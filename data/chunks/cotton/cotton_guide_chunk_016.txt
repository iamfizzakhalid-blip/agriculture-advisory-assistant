Timely picking as bolls open is important to minimize losses and quality deterioration from prolonged field exposure to rain, dew, wind, and soil contamination, all of which can lower lint grade and market value. Picked seed cotton should be kept clean and dry, with prompt transport to ginning facilities to minimize quality loss from moisture absorption, contamination, or extended storage of unginned seed cotton. GINNING AND POST-HARVEST PROCESSING After picking, seed cotton is processed at a ginning facility, where the ginning process mechanically separates the lint (fiber) from the cottonseed. The lint is baled for sale to the textile industry, while cottonseed is processed further to extract cottonseed oil (used as edible oil) and cottonseed cake/meal (used as a protein-rich livestock feed ingredient), with the remaining seed hulls and linters having various industrial uses. Fiber quality assessed at or after ginning includes staple length (fiber length, a major determinant of price and spinning suitability), fiber strength, fineness (micronaire value, related to fiber maturity and thickness), color grade, and trash/contamination content, all of which are affected by variety, growing conditions, picking practices, and post-harvest handling. CROP ROTATION AND CROPPING SYSTEMS Cotton is commonly rotated with other crops such as wheat, maize, sugarcane, or legume/pulse crops depending on the region and cropping calendar. Crop rotation helps reduce the buildup of soil-borne diseases such as Fusarium and Verticillium wilts, which can persist in soil for extended periods under continuous cotton cultivation, and helps manage certain soil-dwelling pests and weed populations associated with continuous cotton monocropping.
