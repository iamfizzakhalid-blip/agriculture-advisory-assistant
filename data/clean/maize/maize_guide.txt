Maize, also called corn and known scientifically as Zea mays, is a tall annual plant belonging to the grass family. It is a single-stemmed crop that usually grows between one and a half to three meters tall, depending on the variety and growing conditions. Maize is what is known as a C4 plant, which means it photosynthesizes very efficiently in hot, sunny weather and uses water more efficiently than crops like wheat or rice. This is part of why maize does so well in warm climates with plenty of sunlight. The plant has a fibrous root system, and as it grows taller it also develops brace roots, sometimes called prop roots, which emerge from the lower part of the stem above the soil surface and help anchor the plant so it does not fall over in wind or heavy rain. The stem is solid and divided into nodes and internodes, and the leaves are long and strap shaped, arranged alternately along the stem. At the very top of the plant is the tassel, which is the male flower structure and produces pollen. Lower down, growing out from a leaf joint, is the ear or cob, which is the female flower structure. The ear is wrapped in husk leaves, and from the tip of the ear, fine silk threads emerge; each individual silk is connected to one potential kernel on the cob, and that kernel can only develop if its silk receives a grain of pollen. Because maize carries separate male and female flower parts on the same plant, and because it depends mainly on wind to carry pollen from the tassel down to the silks, it is considered a cross pollinated crop, and this has practical implications, such as needing some distance between different maize varieties if a farmer wants to keep seed pure, and the fact that pollination problems, like the tassel shedding pollen at a different time than the silks emerge, can lead to poor kernel formation and lower yields.

The maize plant passes through a fairly well defined life cycle that is often described in growth stages. In the early part of its life, called the vegetative stage, the plant is simply putting out leaves and building its root system and stalk strength, and this stage is often tracked by counting how many leaves have fully emerged. During this vegetative period, the plant is also quietly determining how many rows of kernels its future ear will have, so stress during this window, though generally less damaging than stress later on, can still reduce the plant's potential. Eventually the plant reaches tasseling, when the male tassel emerges fully at the top, and shortly after that comes silking, when the silks emerge from the ears and pollination takes place, usually over about a week. This silking period is widely considered the single most critical stage in the entire life of the crop, because if the weather is extremely hot or dry at this exact time, pollen can lose viability or the timing between pollen shedding and silk emergence can fall out of sync, and either problem directly reduces the number of kernels that form, which means fewer kernels means lower yield no matter how well the rest of the season goes. After successful pollination, the kernels pass through a blister stage where they are small and filled with watery fluid, then a milk stage where the inside of the kernel looks milky white, then a dough stage where that milky fluid thickens into a paste, and then a dent stage, named for the small dent that forms at the crown of each kernel as it begins to dry and fill with starch. Eventually the crop reaches what is called physiological maturity, sometimes described by farmers as the black layer stage, because a small blackish layer forms where the kernel attaches to the cob. At this point the kernel has reached its maximum dry weight and stopped accumulating any more yield, even though the grain still contains a fairly high amount of moisture, often around thirty to thirty five percent, and everything that happens after this point, whether in the field or after harvest, is really just about drying the grain down rather than adding any more yield to it.

When it comes to climate, maize grows best when average temperatures sit somewhere between about twenty one and thirty degrees Celsius. Seeds germinate poorly if soil temperatures drop below about ten degrees Celsius, and on the other end, temperatures above about thirty five degrees Celsius, especially if they coincide with dry conditions during flowering, can seriously damage pollination and yield. Over the course of a full season, maize typically needs somewhere between five hundred and eight hundred millimeters of water, though this varies depending on the variety's duration and the local climate, and the timing of that water matters enormously, since the plant can tolerate some dryness during its early vegetative growth but is very sensitive to water shortages during silking and during the grain filling period that follows. Because it is a C4 plant built for capturing sunlight efficiently, maize performs poorly in shade and generally needs full sun exposure to reach good yields. It is also frost sensitive at every stage of growth, so a late frost in spring or an early frost in autumn can both cause serious damage depending on when they occur relative to the crop's stage.

In terms of soil, maize prefers deep, well drained loamy soils that are rich in organic matter, with an ideal soil pH somewhere between about five point eight and seven, which is to say slightly acidic to neutral. In soils that are more acidic than that, aluminum toxicity can become a problem and phosphorus becomes less available to the plant, so many farmers apply lime to correct very acidic soils before planting maize. Maize has quite poor tolerance for waterlogging, and even standing water for as little as one or two days during early growth can starve the roots of oxygen and kill a significant portion of the stand, so drainage is an important consideration, particularly on heavier clay soils. Sandy soils, by contrast, drain too freely and tend to need more frequent irrigation and fertilizer application because they cannot hold water or nutrients as well.

There are many different types of maize suited to different purposes. Dent corn is the most widely grown type worldwide and is mainly used for animal feed, starch production, and ethanol, and it gets its name from the dent that forms in the kernel as it dries. Flint corn has a harder, smoother kernel and is more traditionally grown in parts of Latin America and Europe. Sweet corn is harvested while still immature, at the milk stage, because at that point it has much higher sugar content and is eaten fresh or canned as a vegetable rather than being left to dry down like grain corn. Popcorn has a small, hard kernel with a tough outer layer that allows it to pop when heated, and flour corn has a soft starchy kernel that is easy to grind. There is also waxy corn, which has a different starch composition used for certain industrial purposes. Separately from these kernel types, farmers also choose between open pollinated varieties and hybrids. Open pollinated varieties allow farmers to save their own seed from one year to plant the next, and while they tend to yield somewhat less than hybrids, they are cheaper because seed does not need to be repurchased every season. Hybrid maize is created by crossing two or more specifically bred parent lines, and the resulting seed shows what is called hybrid vigor, meaning noticeably higher yield, more uniform plants, and often better tolerance to stress. However, if a farmer saves seed from a hybrid crop and replants it the following year, that second generation will not breed true, and yields and uniformity will drop noticeably, which is why farmers growing hybrids are generally advised to purchase fresh seed each season rather than saving their own. In some countries, genetically modified maize varieties are also approved for use, most commonly engineered for resistance to certain insect pests or for tolerance to specific herbicides, though the legality and availability of these varieties varies a great deal from country to country.

Preparing the land properly before sowing makes a real difference to how well maize establishes. This typically involves deep plowing every couple of years to break up compacted soil layers, followed by one or two rounds of harrowing to create a fine, crumbly seedbed that allows for even seed depth and good germination. Leveling the field helps water move evenly across it during irrigation and also helps prevent water from pooling in low spots. In areas with heavy rainfall or flooding risk, farmers often form ridges and furrows or raised beds to keep the root zone from becoming waterlogged. Many farmers also work farmyard manure or compost into the soil before sowing, which builds up organic matter and improves the soil's ability to hold both water and nutrients over time.

When it comes to actually sowing the crop, seed rate is usually somewhere around eight to ten kilograms per acre for typical dent hybrids at normal spacing, though this can vary depending on seed size, germination percentage, and how dense a stand the farmer is aiming for. A common spacing recommendation is around sixty to seventy five centimeters between rows and twenty to twenty five centimeters between individual plants within a row, though some hybrids are specifically bred to tolerate higher planting densities than this. Seeds are usually placed around three to five centimeters deep, going a bit deeper in dry or sandy soil to reach residual moisture, and a bit shallower in heavier or wetter soil. Sowing can be done by hand, with a seed drill, or with a precision planter, and precision planting tends to give more even emergence and spacing, which generally translates into better yields because the plants compete less with each other and use light and nutrients more efficiently. The exact timing of sowing depends heavily on the local climate and rainfall pattern, but the general goal is to time planting so that the critical flowering period does not coincide with the hottest or driest part of the season. Treating seed with a fungicide or insecticide before sowing is also common practice, since it protects the young seedling from soil borne diseases and early pests like termites or wireworms during the vulnerable germination period.

Maize is a heavy feeder, and of all the nutrients it needs, nitrogen is the most critical and the one most likely to limit yield if it runs short. A total nitrogen application of around one hundred twenty to one hundred fifty kilograms per hectare is common for well managed hybrid maize, and rather than applying it all at once, it is usually split into two or three doses, typically some at sowing, some around the time the plant is knee high, and some just before tasseling, both to match the plant's actual uptake pattern over time and to reduce the amount of nitrogen that gets lost to leaching. A farmer can often recognize nitrogen deficiency by yellowing that starts at the tip of the older, lower leaves and moves inward along the midrib in a distinctive V shape. Phosphorus is especially important early in the plant's life for root development, and because it does not move around easily in the soil, it is usually applied entirely at the time of sowing rather than split into later doses; a phosphorus deficient plant often shows a purplish discoloration on its leaves, especially in cold soil conditions. Potassium is important for keeping the stalk strong and helping the plant resist disease and fill grain properly, and a shortage of potassium typically shows up as yellowing or scorching along the margins of the older leaves. Zinc deficiency is also extremely common in maize growing regions and is easy to recognize because it produces broad pale or whitish bands running along the midrib of young leaves, something farmers sometimes call white bud, and this is usually corrected by applying zinc sulfate. Other nutrients like sulfur, magnesium, and boron can also become limiting in certain soils, though less commonly than the ones already mentioned, and continuing to add organic matter through farmyard manure or compost helps improve how efficiently the crop uses whatever fertilizer is applied.

Water management is just as important as nutrient management. If rainfall is not reliable, irrigation becomes especially critical at a few key points in the crop's life: during germination and establishment, around the knee high vegetative stage, during tasseling and silking, and during grain filling afterward. Of all of these, the two week window right around silking is by far the most sensitive, and allowing the crop to run short of water during this narrow period can cause yield losses far greater than the same level of water stress would cause at almost any other stage. At the same time, farmers need to avoid the opposite problem of waterlogging, since maize cannot tolerate standing water for more than a day or two, particularly during germination and the early vegetative stages, without suffering significant root damage and stand loss. Depending on the region and the resources available, irrigation might be delivered through furrows, sprinklers, drip systems, which are becoming more common because they use water efficiently, or the crop may simply be grown as a rainfed crop relying entirely on natural rainfall.

Weeds are a major concern in maize, particularly during the first thirty to forty five days after sowing, which is often referred to as the critical weed free period, because young maize plants compete very poorly against weeds for light, water, and nutrients during this window. Once the maize canopy closes and begins shading the ground, it becomes much more capable of suppressing weeds on its own. Farmers control weeds using a combination of approaches, including pre-emergence herbicides applied right after sowing before weeds have a chance to germinate, post-emergence herbicides applied later that are selective enough to kill young weeds without harming the maize itself, mechanical methods like inter-row cultivation or hand hoeing, and sometimes mulching to physically suppress weed growth. The specific weeds present and the herbicides available vary a great deal by region, so herbicide choice needs to match both the weeds actually present in the field and the safety profile for maize itself.

On the pest side, one of the most significant threats to maize in recent years has been fall armyworm, a caterpillar pest that spread from the Americas into Africa and Asia and can cause severe damage if not caught early; its larvae feed inside the whorl of young plants, leaving behind a characteristic ragged, window pane pattern of holes in the leaves, and management usually relies on regular scouting to catch infestations early, targeted insecticide use, encouraging natural predators, or planting resistant Bt hybrids where they are available and legal. Stem borers are another common pest, with their larvae tunneling into the stalk, which in young plants can kill the central growing shoot, a symptom farmers often call dead heart, and in older plants can weaken the stalk enough that it breaks or falls over later in the season. Corn earworm, sometimes called cob borer, feeds directly on the developing kernels within the ear itself. Termites can damage roots and stems, especially in drier conditions, and aphids, besides sucking sap directly from the plant, can also spread viral diseases and, if they build up heavily on the tassels, can interfere with pollen shedding. Rodents and birds can also cause meaningful losses, particularly by eating newly sown seed before it germinates and by damaging ears as the crop approaches maturity. In general, an integrated approach that combines regular field scouting, resistant varieties, conservation of natural predators, and the use of pesticides only when pest numbers cross an economically damaging threshold tends to work better in the long run than routine blanket spraying.

Diseases are another major factor in maize production. A number of fungal leaf diseases, including common rust and various leaf blights and leaf spots, show up as lesions, streaks, or raised pustules on the leaves, and severe infections reduce the amount of healthy leaf area available for photosynthesis, which directly cuts into yield; these are generally managed through resistant varieties, rotating maize with other crops, managing crop residue that can harbor disease, and fungicide application when infection is severe. Stalk rots, caused by various fungi or bacteria, weaken the stalk from the inside, making the plant more prone to lodging and reducing how well it can fill grain, and these problems tend to be worse when the plant is also dealing with a nutrient imbalance, especially low potassium, or drought stress. Ear rots and cob rots are a particular concern because, beyond just spoiling the grain, some of the fungi responsible can produce toxic compounds known as mycotoxins, including aflatoxin and fumonisin, which are genuinely dangerous to the health of both humans and livestock if contaminated grain ends up being consumed, and this risk is especially high when grain is stored in warm, humid conditions without proper drying. Downy mildew is a serious problem in some tropical growing regions, causing stunted growth and a streaked, pale pattern on the leaves. Various viral diseases, usually spread by insects like leafhoppers or aphids, can also cause stunting and a mottled, mosaic pattern in the leaves, and since these are hard to treat directly once a plant is infected, management focuses mainly on controlling the insect vectors and choosing resistant varieties.

Beyond diseases and pests, maize can also suffer from a range of problems that are not caused by any living organism but instead come from stress or imbalance. Barren stalks, meaning plants that produce no ear at all, are usually the result of overcrowding, severe stress right around flowering time, or a serious nitrogen shortage. Poor kernel set, sometimes described as tip back because the missing kernels are concentrated near the tip of the ear, typically results from drought or heat stress during silking, which prevents the later-emerging silks near the tip from being pollinated in time. Lodging, where stalks fall over, can result from weak stalks due to disease or potassium deficiency, from strong winds, from planting the crop too densely, or from applying a lot of nitrogen without enough potassium to balance it, since potassium is what gives the stalk much of its structural strength.

When the crop is ready, harvesting decisions depend on what the maize is being grown for. Grain maize destined for storage and sale is usually harvested once it reaches about twenty to twenty five percent moisture and is then dried further, either naturally in the field or with mechanical drying, down to around twelve to fourteen percent moisture, which is considered safe for storage without significant risk of mold growth. Maize intended for silage, which is fermented whole plant fodder for livestock, is harvested earlier, generally somewhere between the dough and early dent stages, when the whole plant has the right balance of moisture and sugar content for good fermentation. Sweet corn, as mentioned earlier, is harvested much earlier still, at the milk stage, while the kernels are tender and sweet and before they begin drying down and turning starchy. Harvesting itself can be done by hand, picking ears individually, or with a mechanical combine harvester fitted with a corn head, which both picks and shells the grain in one pass.

After harvest, proper drying down to around twelve to fourteen percent moisture before storage is absolutely critical, since grain that goes into storage too wet is highly prone to mold growth and the associated risk of mycotoxin contamination discussed earlier. Stored grain should be kept in clean, dry, pest proof bins or containers, and it is generally best practice to avoid mixing newly harvested grain with older stored grain. Regular monitoring for storage pests like weevils and grain borers is also important, and appropriate storage protectants can be used where infestations are a recurring problem. Shelling, which means removing the kernels from the cob, is usually done once the grain has dried sufficiently, and can be accomplished by hand, with a simple hand-cranked sheller, or with a mechanical sheller for larger quantities.

Maize is used in an enormous number of ways once it leaves the field. As human food, it is eaten as whole grain, ground into various types of flour or meal used in breads, porridges, and traditional dishes around the world, processed into breakfast cereals, and, in the case of sweet corn, eaten fresh as a vegetable. Globally, one of the single largest uses of maize is as animal feed, both as dried grain and as silage, for cattle, poultry, and pigs. Industrially, maize is processed into starch, into corn syrup and other sweeteners, and into ethanol for use as a biofuel, and the germ of the kernel can also be pressed for corn oil. The whole plant, whether used fresh or preserved as silage, also serves directly as fodder for livestock.

Finally, from a farming and economic point of view, the major input costs in maize production are seed, which is a recurring cost every season for farmers using hybrids, fertilizer, irrigation where it is used, labor and machinery, and crop protection products like pesticides and herbicides. Yield potential varies enormously depending on how the crop is managed; a subsistence farmer relying purely on rainfall and minimal inputs might harvest somewhere around one to three tons per hectare, while a well managed irrigated system using good hybrid seed and proper fertilization can exceed eight to twelve tons per hectare or even more. Rotating maize with legume crops like soybean or various beans is a widely recommended practice, both because it helps break the life cycles of pests and diseases that build up when maize is grown continuously in the same field, and because legumes can add nitrogen back into the soil, which benefits the following maize crop. Market timing is also a practical consideration for farmers, since grain prices often drop right after the regional harvest season due to a glut of supply all coming to market at once, so farmers who are able to store their grain safely, meaning properly dried and protected from pests and moisture, often have the option of waiting and selling later in the season when prices may have recovered.
