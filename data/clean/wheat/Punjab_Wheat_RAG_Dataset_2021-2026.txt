SECTION A: YEAR-BY-YEAR TIMELINE (2021–2026)
=== YEAR 2021 (Rabi 2020–21 crop / harvested 2021) ===
[A1] Production baseline entering 2021–22 planning cycle The Wheat Review Committee
(which met in September 2021) noted that Punjab's 2020–21 wheat production stood at 20.9
million tonnes, and directed the province to raise this to 23 million tonnes for the following
(2021–22) crop season [25]. Nationally, Pakistan's 2020 wheat output (harvested June 2020)
was officially estimated at a below-average 25.25 million tonnes, largely reﬂecting reduced
yields due to heavy rains and hail in March–April 2020 in Punjab province [29].
[A2] National yield ﬁgure, 2020/21 marketing year USDA FAS data records the national
wheat yield for the 2020/21 marketing year at 2.9 tons/hectare [1][23].
[A3] Sowing-season input crisis heading into the 2021–22 crop Going into the 2021–22
sowing season, the federal government targeted 30 million tonnes of national wheat
production and asked provinces to increase area and per-acre yield using rust-resistant,
certiﬁed seed, along with proper water, fertilizer, and pesticide usage [25]. However, wheat
lost acreage during late sowing when DAP and urea shortages developed, diverting some
growers to alternative crops such as potatoes and oilseeds [25].
=== YEAR 2022 (Rabi 2021–22 crop, harvested spring 2022) ===
[A4] Punjab sowing shortfall for the 2021–22 crop Punjab, which contributes around 76%
of Pakistan's total wheat production, sowed only 16.21 million acres against a target of 16.67
million acres — a shortfall of over 500,000 acres — for the 2021–22 season [25]. Certiﬁed
seed availability (public and private sector combined) covered only about 25% of
requirement, roughly 530,000 tonnes of seed, including 160,000 tonnes of rust-tolerant
varieties; the remainder of demand was met from farmers' own retained seed stock [25].
[A5] Minimum support price context, 2021–22 A decision on the minimum support price
for the 2021–22 crop was taken late in the season and was set to match Sindh's price of Rs.
2,200 per 40 kg [25]. Over 70% of Punjab's wheat crop that season was reportedly sown
within the most suitable window — mid-November to mid-December [25].
[A6] Record yield achieved in South Punjab, 2021–22 The Secretary of Agriculture for
South Punjab reported that wheat production in South Punjab increased by an average of
2.73% during 2021–22 despite a 2.41% reduction in cultivated area; regional production rose
from 9.239 million metric tons to 9.262 million metric tons. Per-acre yield rose from 34 to
34.92 maunds per acre — described as the ﬁrst-ever record production achieved in South
Punjab [21].
[A7] National area and production, 2021–22 The national wheat-growing area for the
2021–22 season was 8.99 million hectares, with production of 26.39 million tons [14][29].
Separately, the average annual growth in wheat production over the preceding decade
(through 2022–23) was calculated at 0.9%, driven mainly by yield improvements of a
similar magnitude, with cultivated area remaining largely unchanged [20].
[A8] Prior-season import requirement (context carried into 2022) In the season prior to
2021–22, Pakistan harvested a record 28.75 million tonnes of wheat but still had to import
2.2 million tonnes to meet local requirements and build a 1 million tonne strategic reserve; a
15% yield loss was projected to limit output to no more than 25 million tonnes that year [25].
Trading Corporation of Pakistan (TCP) received import offers between US$369–383 per
tonne (cost and freight included) around this period [25].
[A9] National yield ﬁgure, 2021/22 marketing year USDA FAS data records national
wheat yield for 2021/22 at 3.0 tons/hectare [1][23].
[A10] The August 2022 ﬂoods and their agricultural impact Catastrophic monsoon
ﬂooding struck Pakistan from June to October 2022, killing 1,739 people and causing
roughly US$40 billion in damage — described as the deadliest ﬂood event since the 2020
South Asian ﬂoods and the worst in Pakistan's history [52]. FAO assessed that
approximately 9.4 million acres of crop area nationwide were potentially inundated in
August 2022 alone, including 2.7 million acres in Punjab (also 4.8 million acres in Sindh, 1.2
million acres in Balochistan, and 714,000 acres in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa) [50][55]. In
Punjab speciﬁcally, 223 people died, 3,858 were injured, and roughly 7,200 km² (1.78 million
acres) of crops and orchards were affected [52]. More than 1.1 million livestock were killed
nationally, including over 205,100 in Punjab [55]. Nationally, the ﬂoods destroyed 2.6
million hectares of standing Kharif-season crops across Sindh, Balochistan, and Punjab
[43]. Punjab's ﬂood-exposed crop shares were signiﬁcant: the province accounts for
roughly 16% of national wheat production, 42% of rice, 23% of cotton, and 31% of
sugarcane [51].
[A11] Rabi 2022–23 planting despite ﬂood disruption Standing ﬂoodwaters reduced
planted area for Rabi crops in localized low-lying regions of Sindh and Balochistan, and
there were concerns that seed/fertilizer shortages and ﬂood damage to machinery and
irrigation infrastructure would further disrupt planting nationally. However, ﬂoodwaters
receded in time for planting in most areas, and coordinated government and donor efforts
ensured adequate seed and fertilizer supply, resulting in near-average plantings for the
2022–23 Rabi season [43].
=== YEAR 2023 (Rabi 2022–23 crop, harvested spring 2023) ===
[A12] National production and target, 2022–23 The 2022–23 wheat production target was
28.37 million tons; actual estimated production came in at 28.16–28.18 million tonnes, up
from 26.2 million tonnes the prior year — a 7.45% (or, per a separate estimate, 7.5%) year-
on-year increase [19][20]. Sown area increased to 9 million hectares (+0.62%) versus 8.9
million hectares the previous year. The increase was attributed to good weather conditions
and timely sowing [19].
[A13] Minimum support prices, 2022–23 Punjab's minimum support price for the 2022–23
crop was set at Rs. 4,000 per 40 kg, while Sindh announced a slightly higher support price
of Rs. 4,100 per 40 kg [19].
[A14] Decade-average context (2020–21 to 2022–23) Averaged across 2020–21 through
2022–23, wheat was cultivated on 9.1 million hectares nationally, with average production of
about 27.3 million tonnes. The crop accounted for approximately 7.8% of value-added in
agriculture and about 1.9% of national GDP [20].
[A15] Domestic price surge, 2022–early 2023 Domestic wheat ﬂour prices rose sharply
through 2022 and into early 2023, reaching record highs by June 2023. This price surge was
driven by tight market supply, high production and transport costs, and a sharp increase in
the minimum support price (MSP) in early 2023. Currency depreciation exceeding 70%
between January 2022 and September 2023 made imports signiﬁcantly more expensive,
further amplifying domestic price increases [38].
[A16] Import requirement, 2023–24 marketing year Despite Pakistan's traditional status
as a wheat exporter, large quantities were imported over multiple consecutive marketing
years to shore up supply and temper prices; for 2023/24 (April–March), wheat imports were
forecast at 1.8 million tonnes — above the ﬁve-year average but well below the volumes
imported between 2020/21 and 2022/23 [38].
[A17] National yield ﬁgure, 2022/23 marketing year USDA FAS data records national
wheat yield for 2022/23 at 2.9 tons/hectare [1][23].
[A18] Food insecurity context, late 2023 Acute food insecurity (IPC Phase 3/Crisis and
above) affected an estimated 11.8 million people between November 2023 and January
2024, up from 10.5 million between April and October 2023 — driven mainly by high food
prices eroding purchasing power, compounded by lingering effects of the 2022 ﬂoods [38].
=== YEAR 2024 (Rabi 2023–24 crop, harvested spring 2024) ===
[A19] Cost of production detail, 2023–24 crop The Agricultural Policy Institute's cost-of-
production survey (conducted May–June 2023 across major wheat-growing areas of Punjab
and Sindh) found the market/procurement-center-level cost of production at
approximately Rs. 3,303.52 per 40 kg — a 32.73% (Rs. 1,081.33) increase over the prior year,
driven primarily by fertilizer/FYM, harvesting/threshing, plant protection, and
interculture/ploughing costs [20]. About 87% of national wheat production came from
irrigated conditions [20].
[A20] Support price decision, 2023–24 crop The Economic Coordination Committee
(ECC) of the cabinet approved a support price of Rs. 3,900 per 40 kg for the 2023–24 wheat
crop on 20 December 2023; Sindh subsequently revised its own support price upward to Rs.
4,000 per 40 kg [34]. Farmers receiving the support price during the April–June 2023 post-
harvest period were paid Rs. 3,900 per 40 kg equivalent [20].
[A21] Estimated provincial production breakdown, 2023–24 Officials projected Punjab
would produce about 22 million tons of wheat for 2023–24, with Sindh expected to reap
about 4 million tons, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa 1.4 million tons, and Balochistan 1.3 million
tons — implying a national total around 29 million tons, roughly 3 million tons below the 32
million ton target, attributed to elevated input costs (notably a doubling of urea prices due
to alleged price manipulation, which prompted a case referred to the Competition
Commission of Pakistan) [34].
[A22] National wheat estimate for the 2023 calendar-year cropping season The 2023
cropping season (ﬁnalized December 2023) produced an estimated above-average 28.1
million tonnes of wheat nationally, within an overall cereal production total of about 52
million tonnes [38].
[A23] Weather conditions during the 2023–24 growing season Despite warmer-than-
average temperatures in December 2023 and January 2024, vegetation conditions were
average to above average across main wheat-growing areas due to sufficient irrigation
water supply. In minor rainfed ("barani") tracts — including parts of northern Punjab,
southwest Balochistan, and southeast Sindh (together about 10% of total wheat sowings)
— low rainfall negatively affected crop emergence and early development [37][38].
[A24] National yield ﬁgure, 2023/24 marketing year USDA FAS data records national
wheat yield for 2023/24 at 3.1 tons/hectare [1][23].
[A25] Record 2024 harvest and area context For the 2024–25 (harvested-2024) season,
planted area reached approximately 9 million hectares (some sources cite up to 9.1–9.2
million hectares), up from 8.86 million hectares planted for the 2023 harvest, and slightly
higher than the roughly 9 million hectares a year prior [31]. Pakistan achieved a record
wheat output of approximately 31.6 million metric tons in the 2024–25 season [8][39] (a
separate USDA ﬁgure cites 31.8 million tons for the same record season [2]; ﬁgures vary
slightly by reporting agency and revision cycle).
[A26] Fertilizer market disruption entering the 2024–25 crop Cumulative fertilizer
purchases during the 2024 growing season were about 3.2% lower than the previous year.
While phosphate and potash sales rose 8.3% and 45.9% respectively, nitrogen (urea) sales
fell 6.4% due to price gouging — urea prices reportedly doubled during the season.
Persistently high urea prices, combined with dry conditions in December and January,
resulted in less-than-ideal fertilizer application at critical points in the crop cycle, likely
causing yield penalties [31].
[A27] Support price and market structure change, 2024 Prior to the 2023–24 marketing
year, the state-owned Trading Corporation of Pakistan (TCP) purchased most of the
country's wheat import requirement. This changed in July 2023 when the government
allowed private-sector duty-free wheat imports; private-sector import approvals were
reportedly halted as of 31 March 2024, with no clear indication (as of April 2024) that they
would resume for 2024–25 [31]. In Punjab, the support price for the 2024 harvest was
around Rs. 3,900 per 40 kg (~A$545/t at the time), while Sindh's was Rs. 4,000 per 40 kg
(~US$559/t); farmers protested, demanding an increase to Rs. 5,000 per 40 kg and a higher
procurement target (from 2 million to 5 million tonnes) [31].
[A28] Punjab government exits wheat procurement, 2024 The Punjab provincial
government — which had historically purchased wheat from farmers at harvest time at a
guaranteed minimum support price — decided not to procure wheat from the 2024 harvest.
This absence of the largest traditional buyer increased market supply and put additional
downward pressure on prices [37].
[A29] Consumption and per-capita ﬁgures, 2024–25 Pakistan's per-capita wheat
consumption is around 124 kg per year, among the highest in the world. Population growth
combined with continued consumer preference for wheat-based products was projected to
push domestic demand 2.3% higher for 2024–25, to 30.9 million tonnes. Total stockfeed-
industry use was estimated at only 1.9 million tonnes (6.1% of demand), with the poultry
sector accounting for more than 80% of that portion [31]. About 1,000 privately held ﬂour
mills produce roughly 40% of ﬂour consumption nationally, with the balance met by at-
home processing [3].
[A30] Wheat imports and carry-in stocks, 2024–25 outlook (as forecast in April 2024)
Wheat imports for the 2023–24 season totaled 2.8 million tonnes through the end of
February 2024, with a further 500,000 tonnes discharged in March; the government
indicated imports would stop at the start of April 2024. USDA/FAS projected 2024–25 carry-
in stocks close to a record 4.7 million tonnes (up from 3.9 million tonnes the prior season),
well above the government's minimum strategic-reserve requirement of 2.5 million tonnes.
Given record production and higher opening stocks, 2024–25 imports were projected to
drop signiﬁcantly, to around 1 million tonnes [31]. A later (2025) USDA update conﬁrmed
the 2024/25 wheat production estimate was unchanged at 31.4 million tons at that point in
the revision cycle [35].
=== YEAR 2025 (Rabi 2024–25 crop into 2025/26 planning) ===
[A31] Price collapse following the 2024/25 record harvest Wheat prices declined sharply
during the 2024/25 harvest season and remained low afterward. The record 2024/25 harvest
combined with large import volumes led to high inventories, driving prices down further;
the government's decision to abandon its domestic wheat support-price policy exacerbated
the decline. Prices stabilized relatively from around August 2024 onward. By March 2025,
prices were about 35% lower than the high levels of a year earlier; domestic wheat prices in
mid-March 2025 were around US$245 per ton, with ﬂour prices around US$317 per ton [3]
[36].
[A32] Wheat and ﬂour price trend, August 2024 detail Domestic wheat ﬂour prices
declined sharply between March and August 2024 due to abundant market supply from the
record 2024 harvest, compounded by the Punjab government's decision not to procure
from that harvest. By August 2024, prices averaged 35% below the elevated levels of a year
earlier [37].
[A33] Rice and other crop context for the 2024/25 season (cross-reference) Rice
production for 2024/25 was adjusted down to 9.76 million tons due to harvested-area
revisions, versus an earlier unchanged forecast of a record 10 million tons that had
beneﬁted from timely, widespread rains and improved irrigation water availability [35][36].
[A34] Sowing environment entering the 2025/26 crop (autumn 2025) Farmers began
planting the 2025/26 crop amid an uncertain policy environment. Government
procurement — historically dominated by the Punjab Government — was absent for the
2024/25 crop, and there was no clarity on a government-guaranteed price for the 2025/26
crop, creating uncertainty that directly affected planting decisions. With limited winter-
crop alternatives (mainly canola or sesame), most farmers proceeded despite the
uncertainty. Monsoon rains in 2025 were adequate to ﬁll Pakistan's two large reservoirs
(Mangla and Tarbela), ensuring sufficient irrigation water supply for the wheat crop at
sowing time [35].
[A35] Import ban maintained through the 2025/26 marketing year (as of the relevant
USDA update) Pakistan had not imported wheat during the then-current marketing year
due to a standing import ban; the government indicated it might review the ban later in the
marketing year, though imports were considered unlikely. Despite the official export ban
on wheat and ﬂour, unofficial exports to Afghanistan were estimated at 500,000 tons (not
reﬂected in official data) [35].
[A36] Wheat sowing progress and Punjab's target, autumn 2025 By mid-November 2025,
more than 3 million acres had already been sown in Punjab, as the province worked toward
its target of 16.5 million acres for the 2025–26 season. The provincial Agriculture
Department reported no shortage of agricultural inputs, with about 100,000 bags of
certiﬁed wheat seed available through the Punjab Seed Corporation and private-sector
companies. Under a post-ﬂood relief package, the provincial government extended interest-
free loans to farmers for Rabi crops. Improved soil fertility from recent ﬂoods and generally
favorable weather were expected to support good yields — echoing the bumper harvest
recorded after the 2022 ﬂoods [6].
[A37] Recommended high-yield varieties for the 2025–26 Punjab season The Punjab
Agriculture Department recommended the following varieties for 2025–26 sowing: Akbar-
2019, Dilkash-2020, Fakhar-e-Bhakkar, Aruj-2022, Nishan, Pakistan-2013, Faisalabad-2008,
MH-2021, MA-2021, Subhani-2020, and Wafaq-2023 [6].
[A38] Rabi 2025–26 national production target set by the Federal Committee on
Agriculture (FCA) The federal government set a wheat production target of about 30
million tonnes for Rabi 2025–26, relying on adequate water availability in Punjab and Sindh.
This target followed the Rabi 2024–25 target of 27.92 million tonnes, which was exceeded —
actual achieved production was 28.42 million tonnes [10]. (Note: this 28.42 million tonne
ﬁgure for 2024–25 differs from the ~31.6–31.8 million ton "record" ﬁgures cited elsewhere
[2][8][39]; the discrepancy likely reﬂects different measurement bases — target-vs-
achieved accounting by the Ministry of National Food Security and Research versus
USDA/FAS's independently modeled estimate. Both ﬁgures are reported here for
transparency, as instructed.)
[A39] Water allocation for Rabi 2025–26 The IRSA Advisory Committee anticipated no
water shortage for Punjab and Sindh during Rabi 2025–26, allocating 33.814 million acre-
feet (MAF) of water to the two provinces. The Meteorological Department projected below-
normal precipitation for most of the country during November–December 2025, with near-
normal rainfall expected in January 2026 [10].
[A40] Agricultural credit expansion, FY2025 The State Bank of Pakistan set an indicative
agricultural credit disbursement target of Rs. 2.572 trillion for ﬁscal year 2025 — 16.3%
higher than the preceding year's Rs. 2.250 trillion. As of the relevant reporting period, 47
ﬁnancial institutions were providing agricultural loans, including 5 major commercial
banks, 13 medium-sized private banks, 6 Islamic banks, 2 specialized banks (ZTBL and
PPCBL), 11 microﬁnance banks, and 10 microﬁnance institutions/rural support
programmes [10].
=== YEAR 2026 (Rabi 2025–26 crop, harvested spring 2026 — current season as of this
document) ===
[A41] Revised national production estimate, 2025/26 (USDA Grain and Feed Update)
USDA's Grain and Feed Update estimated 2025/26 national wheat production at around 29
million tons — about 8.8% lower than the 2024/25 record of 31.8 million tons. The decline
was attributed to a roughly 5.5% decrease in sown area, driven by the absence of a
government-guaranteed support price, expectations of low market prices, high sowing-
season temperatures, and an extended dry spell [2].
[A42] Earlier, more conservative 2025/26 forecast (USDA Grain and Feed Annual) An
earlier USDA Grain and Feed Annual report forecast 2025/26 production at 27.5 million tons
— about 13% below the 2024/25 record — citing a 7% decrease in sown area and extremely
dry weather. Rainfall was well below average and temperatures signiﬁcantly above average
throughout the growing season [3].
[A43] Punjab-speciﬁc production decline, 2025/26 The decline in national wheat
production for 2025/26 was most pronounced in Punjab province, which alone reportedly
lost around 2 million tons of output relative to the previous year [2].
[A44] Reservoir levels and structural water constraints entering 2025/26 Pakistan's two
major reservoirs, Tarbela and Mangla, were running at critically low levels during the
2024/25 growing season, with some reports describing both as at or near "dead level"
(unable to release water by gravity ﬂow). Since Punjab and Sindh rely heavily on canal
irrigation fed by these reservoirs for their wheat crops, this posed a serious risk. Water
demand has grown substantially since the 1970s, while reservoir storage capacity has
reportedly declined by about one-third due to sedimentation [8].
[A45] Heat stress and grain-ﬁlling impact, 2025/26 Temperatures signiﬁcantly above
average during the 2025/26 season damaged grain-ﬁlling at a critical growth stage.
Agronomic research indicates that even a 1°C rise above the ideal temperature during grain
ﬁlling can reduce yield by 5–7%; farmers in central Punjab and upper Sindh experienced
this kind of damaging heat during the season [8].
[A46] Absence of a pre-season price signal, 2025/26 For the ﬁrst time in many years, the
government failed to announce a clear wheat support price before the October 2024 start of
the 2024–25 sowing season, and this uncertainty persisted into 2025–26 planning; farmers
depend on such price signals to guide planting decisions. Without a guaranteed price, many
farmers judged the risk too high and reduced wheat area. This shift toward market
deregulation was reportedly inﬂuenced partly by IMF reform requirements, removing a
traditional safety net for growers [8].
[A47] Consumption, import, and stocks outlook, 2025/26 National wheat consumption
for 2025/26 was forecast at around 31.9 million tons, driven mainly by population growth;
an estimated 7% of total demand was expected to go to the feed industry, almost entirely to
poultry [3]. Given the gap between forecast production and consumption, import
requirement estimates for 2025/26 ranged from about 1 million tons (based on the higher,
upwardly revised production estimate) [2] to as much as 1.7 million tons (based on more
conservative production assumptions) [8]. Wheat ending stocks for 2025/26 were forecast
to decline to around 2 million tons, down sharply from the record level of nearly 5 million
tons estimated for 2024/25 [2][3].
[A48] Support price and retail price levels, 2025–26 procurement season The
government's official wheat support price for Punjab for the 2025–26 procurement season
was set at Rs. 3,500 per 40 kg bag. Prices in Sindh and Balochistan tended to run somewhat
higher, due to lower local production and higher transport costs. Retail wheat ﬂour (atta)
prices ranged between roughly Rs. 4,500 and Rs. 5,837 per 50 kg pack [8].
[A49] Subsidy and relief measures, 2025–26 The Punjab Food Department reportedly
supplied wheat to ﬂour mills at subsidized rates to help control atta (ﬂour) prices. Subsidies
through the Benazir Income Support Program (BISP) were also under consideration to
protect lower-income households from elevated ﬂour costs [8].
[A50] Government-to-government import negotiations, 2025–26 As of early 2026,
negotiations were reportedly underway with countries including Russia and Kazakhstan
for government-to-government wheat procurement deals at below-market rates, as one
option for meeting the projected 2025/26 import requirement without disrupting domestic
farmgate prices [8].
[A51] National yield ﬁgures, 2024/25 and 2025/26 marketing years USDA FAS data
records national wheat yield at 3.2 tons/hectare for both the 2024/25 and 2025/26 marketing
years — the highest in the entire 2015–2026 historical series tracked by USDA [1][23].
[A52] 2025 monsoon ﬂooding — context and comparison to 2022 Monsoon ﬂooding in
2025 again affected standing Kharif-season crops in Sindh, Balochistan, and Punjab — the
same regions from which the country had not yet fully recovered following the
unprecedented 2022 ﬂoods. However, the overall ﬂooding extent in 2025 was not as severe
as in 2022, particularly in Sindh and Balochistan. Punjab was reported as the most affected
province in the 2025 event, recording the worst ﬂooding there in over four decades; roughly
2.9 million people across Punjab were evacuated as of the reporting period, with numbers
expected to rise as rains continued [43]. Despite the direct Kharif-season ﬂooding impacts
and potential knock-on effects for the Rabi wheat season, adequate wheat reserves carried
over from the previous Rabi season, together with improved macroeconomic conditions
(stronger foreign exchange reserves, a stable rupee, and easing food inﬂation), were
expected to help Pakistan absorb the shock without a repeat of 2022-level food security
impacts; only short-term, localized, acute food insecurity was anticipated in directly
affected areas [43].

SECTION C: WHEAT VARIETIES RELEASED / RECOMMENDED FOR
PUNJAB (with release-era context)
[C1] Institutional variety-release history The Wheat Research Institute (WRI) at Ayub
Agricultural Research Institute (AARI), Faisalabad has released 65 wheat varieties for
various zones since the institute's establishment in 1906 [40][41][42].
[C2] Recent named varieties by release code Conﬁrmed released/recommended varieties
include: Anaj-17, Akbar-19 (Akbar-2019), Dilkash-20 (Dilkash-2020), Subhani-21 (Subhani-
2020/21), MH-21, Arooj-2022 (also styled Aruj-2022), plus barley varieties (Jau-17, Sultan-
17, Talbina-21, Jau-21, Pearl-21) and Durum-21 — Pakistan's ﬁrst pasta/noodle wheat variety
[45].
[C3] MH-21 variety detail MH-21 (varietal code V-12304) is a high-yielding, rust-resistant
bread wheat variety for irrigated areas of Punjab. In preliminary (2011–2012) and regular
(2012–2013) yield trials it outperformed the check variety Faisalabad-08 by 11.2% and
10.28% respectively in grain yield. It was subsequently tested across 21 locations in Punjab
(2013–2014) and 18 locations nationally (2014–2015, 2017–2018, 2019–2020) for yield and
disease stability [41][42].
[C4] Dilkash-20 variety detail Dilkash-20 (V-16005) is a newly approved wheat variety for
Punjab noted for high yielding potential and disease resistance; its development and multi-
location yield trial history (2017 onward, including the Punjab Uniform Wheat Yield Trial
and National Uniform Wheat Yield Trials) is documented in dedicated variety-release
literature [40].
[C5] Varieties recommended speciﬁcally for the 2025–26 Punjab sowing season Akbar-
2019, Dilkash-2020, Fakhar-e-Bhakkar, Aruj-2022, Nishan, Pakistan-2013, Faisalabad-2008,
MH-2021, MA-2021, Subhani-2020, Wafaq-2023 [6].
[C6] Package-of-Practices (Rabi 2025–26) additional varieties and traits Newer entries
in Punjab's official package of practices include specialty end-use types — e.g., a "PBW
Biscuit 1 (2024)" premium cookie/biscuit-quality variety (moderately resistant to yellow
rust, resistant to brown rust, ~154-day maturity, average yield 17.2 quintals/acre); durum
types such as "PDW 291 (2005)" suited for pasta-making, resistant to yellow and brown rust
and to loose smut, with low incidence of yellow berry; and general-purpose high-yield lines
such as "PBW 872 (2025)" (moderately resistant to yellow and brown rust, ~152-day
maturity, bold lustrous grains, average yield 24.4 quintals/acre) and "PBW 824 (2021)"
(resistant to brown rust, moderately resistant to yellow rust, ~158-day maturity, average
yield 23.2 quintals/acre, recommended for Seeder/Super Seeder use in rice-residue-
managed ﬁelds) [13].
[C7] Cultivar selection basis used in current agronomic trials Field research (e.g.,
biochar/irrigation trials at the University of the Punjab, Lahore, 2023–2024) used Dilkash-
2020, Akbar-2019, and FSD-08 as representative cultivars, selecting them per AARI's
recommended cultivar list for irrigated soils [17][49].
SECTION D: THE 2022 FLOODS — DEDICATED DEEP-DIVE (Cross-Year
Reference Topic)
[D1] Scale of the disaster The 2022 Pakistan ﬂoods (June–October 2022) killed 1,739
people and caused approximately US$40 billion in damage, driven by heavier-than-usual
monsoon rains and glacier melt following a severe heatwave — both linked to climate
change. Pakistan declared a state of emergency on 25 August 2022. It was described as the
world's deadliest ﬂood since the 2020 South Asian ﬂoods and the worst ﬂood in Pakistan's
history [52].
[D2] Crop area affected FAO assessed approximately 9.4 million acres of crop area
nationally as potentially inundated in August 2022: 4.8 million acres in Sindh, 2.7 million
acres in Punjab, 1.2 million acres in Balochistan, and 714,000 acres in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa
[50][55]. At least 1.2 million livestock were killed nationally [50].
[D3] Punjab-speciﬁc toll In Punjab, 223 people died and 3,858 were injured; roughly 7,200
km² (1.78 million acres) of crops and orchards were affected. Punjab reported 30% of all
recorded injuries nationally [52].
[D4] Sectoral crop damage nationally The ﬂoods affected nearly 15% of Pakistan's rice
crop and 40% of its cotton crop [50]. Sindh Province alone — the hardest-hit region —
accounts for 42% of national rice production, 23% of cotton, and 31% of sugarcane [51][54].
Punjab's exposed shares were 16% of national wheat production, 42% of rice, 23% of cotton,
and 31% of sugarcane [51].
[D5] Direct inundation extent (satellite-based) Satellite mapping (Sentinel-1, 22–28
August 2022) showed about 2.5 million hectares of land directly inundated in Sindh alone
(over 18% of the province's total area), obstructing access across roughly three-quarters of
the province [54][58].
[D6] Impact on Rabi (wheat) planting for 2022–23 Standing ﬂoodwaters reduced planted
area for Rabi crops in localized low-lying regions of Sindh and Balochistan. There were
concerns that seed and fertilizer shortages, plus ﬂood damage to machinery and irrigation
infrastructure, would further disrupt planting. However, ﬂoodwaters receded in time for
planting in most areas, and coordinated government/donor efforts secured adequate seed
and fertilizer supplies, resulting in near-average plantings for 2022–23 [43].
[D7] Longer-term food security consequences A UNICEF rapid survey across 15 ﬂood-
affected districts (released May 2023) found that one-third of children aged 6–23 months
suffered moderate acute malnutrition, with 14% suffering severe acute malnutrition. FAO
and WFP's June–November 2023 outlook warned that acute food insecurity was likely to
further deteriorate if economic and political conditions worsened, compounding the 2022
ﬂood effects [50].
[D8] Comparison with the 2025 ﬂood event Monsoon ﬂooding recurred in 2025, again
affecting Kharif crops in Sindh, Balochistan, and Punjab — regions that had not yet fully
recovered from 2022. However, 2025's ﬂooding was less severe overall than 2022,
particularly in Sindh and Balochistan, even though Punjab speciﬁcally recorded its worst
ﬂooding in over four decades during the 2025 event [43].
SECTION E: AGRONOMY AND FIELD-LEVEL PRACTICES (Not Year-Speciﬁc
— General Reference)
[E1] Botanical classiﬁcation and cropping system Wheat (Triticum aestivum) belongs to
the Gramineae family and is grown in all four provinces of Pakistan, particularly Punjab
and Sindh, during the winter (Rabi) season [14].
[E2] National average yield by irrigation type Progressive wheat growers in irrigated areas
achieve grain yields of 5–7 tonnes/hectare. However, the national average yield is around
3.0 tons/hectare for irrigated wheat and only about 1.2 tons/hectare for rainfed (barani)
wheat [24].
[E3] Sowing time criteria Sowing time selection depends on soil and climatic conditions
and cropping-zone type. Barani (rainfed) and humid areas require earlier sowing, during
October, before the onset of winter [14].
[E4] Recommended sowing windows by province Punjab: 1–20 November (ideal window;
official target to complete sowing by 30 November). Sindh: 1–30 November. Khyber
Pakhtunkhwa: 15 October–15 November. Late sowing sharply reduces yield — each week of
delay past mid-November can cost roughly 10–12 kg of grain per acre due to compressed
grain-ﬁlling [5].
[E5] Seeding speciﬁcations Ideal seed depth is 1.5 cm, with row-to-row spacing of 25 cm
and plant-to-plant spacing of 10 cm. Target plant population is 9–10 lac (900,000–
1,000,000) per acre, aiming for 30–35 plants per square meter with well-developed, fertile
tillers. Standard seed rate is 50–55 kg/acre via seed drill; for late sowing (mid-December to
end of December), seed rate increases to 60–70 kg/acre to compensate for reduced tillering
under low-temperature conditions [5][14].
[E6] Sowing methods Drill sowing (seed placed at proper depth via seed drill) gives
uniform germination and even plant-stand establishment and is preferred over
broadcasting. Alternative methods include ridge sowing (recommended for clay
loam/heavy loam soils and rainfed areas), furrow-side manual sowing (recommended for
saline/waterlogged soils), and zero-tillage direct sowing into rice ﬁelds without ploughing
(suited to loamy/clayey soils with good drainage), which saves ﬁeld-preparation time and
advances sowing dates [14].
[E7] Micronutrient management — boron Boron deﬁciency is not typically observed in
soils irrigated by canal water with pH below 8, but is a problem in sandy and saline soils.
Deﬁciency disrupts fertilization, reducing grain number and size. Recommended
correction: 3–4 kg of boron (gold boron or boro star) per acre at land preparation, or two
foliar applications of boron (20% at 125 g/acre, or 5% at 500 ml/acre) 15 days apart [14].
[E8] Micronutrient management — zinc and potash Zinc sulfate (33%) at 6 kg/acre and
boric acid (17%) at 2.5 kg/acre are recommended — unless zinc sulfate was already applied
to the preceding crop. Potash application increases stalk length, grain number, grain
weight, and improves grain color and taste [12][14].
[E9] Fertilizer program by soil fertility class (Punjab Agriculture Department guidance)
Weak/poor soils: 2 bags DAP + 2 bags Urea + 1 bag SOP/MOP per acre. Medium fertility soils:
proportionately higher DAP, 2 bags Urea, 1 bag SOP/MOP. Fertile soils: 2 bags DAP + 2 bags
Urea + 1 bag SOP/MOP per acre (adjusted per soil test). Full phosphorus and potash, plus 1/3
of nitrogen, should be applied at sowing (ideally via band placement with a drill);
remaining nitrogen is split across 2–3 later applications. Farmers are urged to have soil
tested at government soil and water testing laboratories before ﬁnalizing doses [12].
[E10] Irrigation requirement Total crop water requirement is typically 16–18 inches, from a
combination of irrigation and rainfall. Pre-sowing irrigation ("rauni") is applied where soil
moisture is inadequate to ensure a ﬁrm seedbed at depth with ﬁne surface tilth for uniform
emergence. Irrigation counts may be reduced on medium-textured soils for certain
varieties to prevent lodging [5][11][13].
[E11] Water-saving techniques Laser land leveling and bed planting improve water-use
efficiency and reduce weed pressure [11].
[E12] Soil amendment research — biochar A ﬁeld trial at the Botanical Garden, University
of the Punjab, Lahore (2023–2024) tested activated acacia biochar (AAB) at 0, 5, and 10
tons/hectare across three cultivars (Dilkash-2020, Akbar-2019, FSD-08) under ﬁve
irrigation regimes (100%, 80%, 70%, 60%, 50% of ﬁeld capacity). The 10-ton AAB
treatment improved soil carbon recovery to 45% and carbon sequestration capacity to 49%,
versus 0.43% and 0.13% respectively for non-amended soil. Microporosity and water
retention improved by 1.1× and 2.2× respectively with 10-ton AAB amendment. Reduced
irrigation regimes negatively affected plant growth and yield across all treatments [17][49].
[E13] Agro-ecological zones (rainfall basis) Punjab wheat-growing districts are classiﬁed
into agro-ecological zones based on average annual rainfall: Cotton zone (~156 mm) — e.g.,
Khanewal, Bahawalpur; Central mixed zone (~446 mm) — e.g., Faisalabad; Rice zone (~800
mm) — e.g., Gujranwala; Arid zone (~900 mm) — e.g., Chakwal [18].
[E14] Soil and climate parameters Recommended soil types range from sandy loam to clay
loam, with ideal pH 6.0–7.5. Optimum sowing temperature is 20–25°C, with cooler
temperatures favored during grain-ﬁlling [11].
SECTION F: DISEASES AND PESTS (General Reference)
[F1] Major fungal/bacterial diseases Yellow rust (stripe rust), brown rust (leaf rust), stem
rust, loose smut, and Karnal bunt are the major wheat diseases in Pakistan [15]. Karnal bunt
in particular is addressed in the current Punjab Package of Practices, which notes several
newer varieties bred with Karnal bunt resistance [13].
[F2] Disease control strategy Rust diseases (yellow, brown, stem) are managed primarily
via resistant/improved varieties. Loose smut and Karnal bunt are controlled through seed
treatment with recommended fungicides prior to sowing [15].
[F3] Wheat aphid population dynamics A multi-season ﬁeld study (2010–2011, ten
experimental farms, no pesticide use) tracked wheat aphid populations and their natural
enemies across four Punjab agro-ecological zones (cotton zone — Khanewal and
Bahawalpur districts; central mixed zone — Faisalabad; rice zone — Gujranwala; arid zone
— Chakwal). The commonly grown variety "Sahar" was drill-sown at 125 kg/ha across all
sites, October 15–31, in both seasons, following local agronomic recommendations [18].
[F4] Other pest pressures Termites (particularly in sandy/barani soils) and armyworms
are additional recurring pest concerns in Pakistani wheat production [11].
SECTION G: RESEARCH INSTITUTIONS AND POLICY BODIES (General
Reference)
[G1] Wheat Research Institute (WRI), Faisalabad Formal wheat research in Punjab traces
to 1906, with the establishment of a Cereal Section at Lyallpur (now Faisalabad), upgraded
to full Wheat Research Institute status in 1975. Subcontinent-wide wheat breeding began
around 1907 with a botanical survey identifying 25 landraces across three wheat species;
this became the base for subsequent breeding, yielding ten improved varieties released
1911–1965 [16][44].
[G2] Ayub Agricultural Research Institute (AARI) AARI originated in 1962 following the
bifurcation of research and education functions from the former Punjab Agricultural
College and Research Institute, Lyallpur (established 1906). Its main campus is in
Faisalabad, with ecological-zone-speciﬁc research institutes, stations, sub-stations, testing
centers, service laboratories, and demonstration farms located throughout Punjab. AARI's
mission centers on developing new varieties and technologies for food safety, food security,
sustainable exportable surplus, value addition, and natural resource conservation [47].
[G3] National production growth since independence Pakistan's national wheat
production has grown from about 2.63 million tonnes in 1947–48 to over 25 million tonnes
in recent decades — attributed to the cumulative effect of improved varieties, irrigation
expansion, and input intensiﬁcation [16][44].
[G4] Policy coordination bodies The Federal Committee on Agriculture (FCA)
coordinates seasonal production targets, seed availability planning, water allocation (in
conjunction with IRSA), and price/support policy across provinces [10]. The Ministry of
National Food Security and Research (MNFSR) and the Pakistan Agricultural Research
Council (PARC) are additional key national institutions involved in wheat policy and
research [9][15].
[G5] Seed production and distribution AARI, Faisalabad provides pre-basic seed of
approved varieties (wheat and other crops) at reasonable rates, along with nursery plants
for horticultural and citrus crops at subsidized rates [43]. The Punjab Seed Corporation and
private-sector companies jointly supply certiﬁed seed for the wheat sowing season [6].
SECTION H: PRICES, ECONOMICS, AND FARM-LEVEL FINANCIALS
(General / Multi-Year Reference)
[H1] Cost of cultivation range Estimated cost of wheat cultivation per acre falls in a broad
range of roughly PKR 32,000–52,000, depending on inputs, region, and farming intensity
[4][5]. A separate 2023 API cost-of-production survey found market/procurement-level
cost of production at Rs. 3,303.52 per 40 kg for the 2023–24 crop, a 32.73% year-on-year
increase [20].
[H2] Proﬁtability and yield per acre One 2026 estimate placed typical net proﬁt at roughly
PKR 26,750 per acre under average conditions [4]. Typical yields for a well-managed ﬁeld
are 35–45 maunds per acre, with high-performing farms reaching 50–60 maunds per acre (1
maund ≈ 37.3 kg) [5].
[H3] Post-harvest losses Post-harvest losses due to poor threshing and storage are
estimated at roughly 10–15% of national production, materially reducing the effective
marketable surplus [11].
[H4] Yield gap versus global average Pakistan's average wheat yield (cited at around 1.8
tons/acre in some estimates) still lags the global average of about 2.8 tons/acre, indicating
substantial room for improvement via agronomy, mechanization, and input-use efficiency
[11].
