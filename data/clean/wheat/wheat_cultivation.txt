Wheat Cultivation Complete Guide: Botany, Varieties, Sowing, Fertilizer, Irrigation, Diseases, Pests, Growth Stages, Harvesting, and Yield Improvement

INTRODUCTION TO WHEAT
Wheat (Triticum aestivum, common/bread wheat; Triticum durum, durum wheat) is one of the world's most important cereal crops and a staple food source for a large share of the global population. It belongs to the grass family Poaceae and the tribe Triticeae. Wheat grain is used to produce flour for bread, chapati, biscuits, pasta, and many other food products, as well as for animal feed and industrial starch production. Wheat straw is also used as animal fodder and bedding. Globally, wheat is grown on more land area than almost any other crop, and it is a rabi (winter) crop in South Asia, sown in autumn/winter and harvested in spring, while in temperate regions it may be grown as either winter wheat (autumn-sown) or spring wheat (spring-sown), depending on climate and variety type.

BOTANICAL CHARACTERISTICS
Wheat is a self-pollinated annual grass. Its root system is fibrous, with both seminal (seed) roots emerging at germination and adventitious (crown) roots developing later from the crown region near the soil surface. The crown roots become the dominant root system and are critical for water and nutrient uptake, which is why the crown root initiation stage is considered so important for irrigation and fertilizer timing. The stem is a hollow culm with several nodes, and the plant produces multiple tillers (side shoots) from the crown, each capable of producing a productive ear (spike) under good management. The inflorescence is a spike (ear) composed of spikelets, each containing florets that develop into grains after pollination. Wheat grain consists of the bran (outer layer, rich in fiber), the endosperm (starchy interior, milled into white flour), and the germ (embryo, rich in oil, protein, and micronutrients).

TYPES AND CLASSIFICATION OF WHEAT
- Bread wheat (Triticum aestivum): The most widely grown type, used for bread, chapati, and general flour products; hexaploid.
- Durum wheat (Triticum durum): Hard wheat with high protein and gluten strength, primarily used for pasta and semolina; tetraploid.
- Winter wheat: Sown in autumn, requires a period of cold temperature (vernalization) to induce flowering, harvested in early-mid summer.
- Spring wheat: Sown in spring, does not require vernalization, harvested in late summer/autumn; grown where winters are too severe for winter wheat to survive.
- Classification by grain hardness and color: hard red winter, hard red spring, soft red winter, hard white, soft white, and durum, each suited to different end uses (bread, pastry, noodles, pasta).

CLIMATE AND SOIL REQUIREMENTS
Wheat grows best in a cool, moist climate during the vegetative growth period and a warm, dry climate during ripening and harvesting. Optimal temperature for germination is around 20-25°C; growth can occur between roughly 3-4°C (minimum) and 30-32°C (maximum), with the ideal range for most growth stages being 15-20°C. Excessively high temperatures (above roughly 30-32°C), particularly during flowering, grain filling, and ripening, cause shriveled grain, reduced grain weight, and yield loss. Frost during flowering can also damage the crop. Adequate but not excessive rainfall or irrigation is required; total water requirement is generally in the range of 400-650 mm depending on climate, evapotranspiration, and variety.
Wheat can be grown on a wide range of soils but performs best on well-drained loamy or clay-loam soils with good water-holding capacity and good structure. Soil pH between 6.0 and 7.5 is considered ideal; strongly acidic or alkaline/saline soils reduce nutrient availability and yield. Waterlogged, poorly drained, or compacted soils are unsuitable and promote root diseases, poor tillering, and lodging. Deep soils with good organic matter content support stronger root systems and higher yield potential.

LAND PREPARATION
Proper land preparation ensures good seed-to-soil contact, adequate root development, and efficient water use. Typical steps include:
1. Deep plowing (once every 2-3 years or after a deep-rooted previous crop) to break hardpan, improve aeration, and bury crop residues and weed seeds.
2. Two to three subsequent harrowings or cultivations with a disc or tine cultivator to break clods, mix in manure, and create a fine, firm seedbed.
3. Planking/leveling the field, which is critical for uniform irrigation water distribution and to prevent water stagnation in low spots and drought stress in high spots.
4. Removing perennial weeds, stubble, and previous crop residues, or incorporating residues well in advance of sowing so they decompose rather than tying up soil nitrogen.
5. Applying farmyard manure (FYM), compost, or green manure before the last plowing to improve soil organic matter, structure, water-holding capacity, and microbial activity.
6. Where laser land leveling technology is available, it significantly improves irrigation efficiency, uniform crop stand, and can improve yield while reducing water use.

WHEAT VARIETIES AND SEED SELECTION
Wheat varieties differ in maturity duration, yield potential, disease resistance, lodging resistance, grain quality, and adaptation to local climate and soil. Key factors farmers should weigh when selecting a variety include:
- Disease resistance, especially to the locally prevalent rust races and smuts, since new rust strains periodically overcome resistance in older varieties.
- Maturity duration (early, medium, or late maturing), which affects sowing/harvest windows and suitability for the following crop in rotation.
- Yield potential under local management and irrigation conditions.
- Lodging resistance, particularly important where high nitrogen and irrigation are used.
- Response to fertilizer and irrigation (some varieties are bred specifically for high-input, high-yield systems).
- Grain quality traits relevant to end use (protein content, gluten strength, grain hardness, milling and baking quality).
- Tolerance to heat stress (important for late-sown crops or regions with rising terminal heat) and tolerance to salinity or drought where relevant.
Farmers should select varieties recommended by local agricultural research institutes or extension services for their specific agro-climatic zone, since varieties are regionally tested. Using certified seed from a reliable, reputable source rather than farm-saved seed from previous harvests is strongly recommended, as certified seed has higher genetic purity, higher germination rates, and is free of seed-borne diseases such as loose smut and Karnal bunt. Seed should also be tested for germination percentage before sowing; a germination rate below about 85% suggests a higher seed rate is needed or the seed lot should be replaced.

SOWING TIME
The optimum sowing time is generally from late October to November in most temperate and subtropical wheat-growing regions, though exact timing depends on local climate, latitude, and the specific variety's maturity type and vernalization requirement. Sowing too early can expose the crop to warmer temperatures that encourage excessive vegetative growth, higher pest pressure (such as aphids and termites), and increased disease risk before winter. Sowing too late (after the optimum window) shortens the vegetative and tillering period, reduces the number of productive tillers, and exposes the crop to high temperatures during flowering and grain filling in spring, which sharply reduces yield through poor grain filling and shriveled grain. Where late sowing is unavoidable, farmers should switch to a variety specifically bred for late sowing/heat tolerance and increase the seed rate to compensate for reduced tillering.

SOWING METHOD AND SEED RATE
Wheat can be sown by broadcasting, drilling with a seed drill, dibbling, or bed planting (raised beds with furrows for irrigation).
- Line/drill sowing in rows is generally preferred over broadcasting because it ensures uniform seed depth and spacing, better and more even germination, easier interculture and weed control, and more efficient irrigation.
- Recommended row-to-row spacing is typically about 20-22.5 cm, though closer spacing (15-18 cm) may be used for late-sown crops to compensate for reduced tillering.
- Seed should be sown at a depth of about 4-5 cm; sowing too deep delays emergence, weakens seedlings, and reduces final stand, while sowing too shallow risks poor anchorage and exposure to drying.
- Standard seed rate is generally around 100-125 kg per hectare for normal, timely sowing, with a somewhat higher rate (up to about 150 kg/ha) for late sowing to compensate for reduced tillering capacity, and a lower rate may be used for early sowing or wider row spacing.
- Bed planting (sowing on raised beds with furrows between them) can improve water use efficiency, reduce lodging, and ease interculture operations such as weeding and top-dressing of fertilizer, compared to flat sowing.
- Seed treatment with a recommended fungicide (and sometimes insecticide) before sowing helps protect against seed-borne and soil-borne diseases and early-season insect pests, and generally improves germination and stand establishment.

CROP GROWTH STAGES
Understanding wheat growth stages helps time irrigation, fertilizer, weed control, and disease/pest scouting correctly:
1. Germination and emergence: Seed absorbs moisture, seminal roots and the first leaf (coleoptile) emerge.
2. Seedling establishment: Early leaf development, crown (adventitious) root system begins forming.
3. Tillering: The plant produces multiple side shoots (tillers) from the crown; this stage largely determines the number of ears (spikes) the plant will eventually produce.
4. Stem elongation/jointing: Internodes elongate, the stem grows upward, and nodes become visible; the growing point rises above the soil surface, after which frost damage becomes more serious.
5. Booting: The flag leaf (last leaf) emerges and the developing ear swells within the flag leaf sheath.
6. Heading/ear emergence: The ear emerges fully from the flag leaf sheath.
7. Flowering (anthesis): Pollination occurs; this is a highly sensitive stage to heat stress, frost, and moisture stress.
8. Grain filling (milk and dough stages): Grain accumulates starch and protein, moving from a watery/milky consistency to a soft dough and then a hard dough; adequate moisture and nutrient supply during this stage strongly affect final grain weight.
9. Physiological maturity: The grain reaches maximum dry weight and the crop turns golden yellow; moisture content is still relatively high, but no further increase in grain weight occurs.
10. Harvest maturity: Grain moisture drops to a level suitable for mechanical harvesting and safe storage.

IRRIGATION REQUIREMENTS AND SCHEDULING
Wheat is sensitive to both water stress and waterlogging, and yield can be significantly reduced by moisture stress at critical growth stages. Key irrigation stages, in order of general importance, are:
1. Crown root initiation stage (about 20-25 days after sowing): Widely regarded as the single most critical irrigation, as it strongly affects root system development, tillering, and ultimately final yield; missing or delaying this irrigation is one of the most damaging management errors in wheat production.
2. Tillering stage (about 40-45 days after sowing): Supports tiller development and canopy establishment.
3. Jointing/stem elongation stage: Supports rapid vegetative growth and ear development within the stem.
4. Flowering/heading stage: Water stress at this stage can cause floret sterility and reduced grain set.
5. Milk stage (early grain filling): Important for establishing grain number and initial grain size.
6. Dough stage (late grain filling): Affects final grain weight and test weight; water stress here causes shriveled, lightweight grain.
Depending on soil type, rainfall, climate, and irrigation method, wheat generally requires about 4-6 irrigations under flood/furrow irrigation systems; sandy soils need more frequent, lighter irrigations, while heavier clay soils can be irrigated less frequently with larger amounts. Sprinkler and drip irrigation systems, where available, can improve water use efficiency considerably compared to flood irrigation, allowing more precise, frequent applications of smaller amounts of water and reducing water losses to runoff, deep percolation, and evaporation. Avoiding water stress during crown root initiation and grain filling stages has the greatest overall impact on final yield; over-irrigation, on the other hand, encourages lodging, waterlogging-related root diseases, and nutrient leaching, particularly nitrogen loss.

FERTILIZER AND NUTRIENT MANAGEMENT
Wheat requires a balanced supply of macronutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium) as well as secondary and micronutrients (sulfur, zinc, manganese, boron, among others) for optimal growth and yield.
- Nitrogen (N): The most yield-limiting nutrient in most wheat production systems; it drives vegetative growth, tillering, leaf area, and ultimately grain protein content. It should be applied in split doses rather than all at once, to reduce losses through leaching, volatilization, and denitrification, and to better match crop demand across growth stages: typically a basal dose at sowing, with the remainder split between the crown root initiation stage and the tillering/jointing stage irrigations. Excess nitrogen without balancing potassium can cause excessive vegetative growth, delayed maturity, lodging, and increased disease susceptibility (especially rust and powdery mildew).
- Phosphorus (P): Essential for early root development, tillering, and energy transfer within the plant; because phosphorus is relatively immobile in soil, it is usually applied fully as a basal dose at or before sowing, placed near the seed for efficient uptake by young roots.
- Potassium (K): Improves water regulation, disease resistance, straw strength (reducing lodging risk), and grain quality; usually applied as a basal dose at sowing, though it can be split on very light or leaching-prone soils.
- Sulfur: Important for protein synthesis and chlorophyll formation; deficiency causes overall yellowing similar to nitrogen deficiency but tends to appear first on younger leaves; commonly supplied through sulfur-containing fertilizers such as ammonium sulfate or gypsum, or through FYM.
- Zinc: A widely deficient micronutrient in many wheat-growing soils, especially calcareous or high-pH soils; deficiency causes stunted growth, interveinal chlorosis (yellowing between leaf veins), and delayed maturity; corrected through soil application of zinc sulfate before sowing or foliar sprays during the crop cycle.
- Manganese and boron deficiencies can also occur in some soils and are usually corrected through targeted foliar sprays based on soil or tissue testing.
- Farmyard manure, compost, and other organic amendments applied before sowing improve soil organic matter, structure, microbial activity, and nutrient-holding capacity over the medium to long term, complementing chemical fertilizers rather than replacing them in high-yield systems.
A soil test before the cropping season is strongly recommended, as exact nutrient requirements vary substantially with soil fertility status, previous cropping history, and organic matter content; blanket fertilizer recommendations are only a starting approximation.

WEED MANAGEMENT
Common weeds competing with wheat include wild oats (Avena fatua and Avena ludoviciana), littleseed canary grass (Phalaris minor), other grassy weeds, and broadleaf weeds such as Chenopodium album (lamb's quarters), Convolvulus arvensis (field bindweed), and Rumex species (docks). Weeds compete directly with wheat for nutrients, water, light, and space, and can cause substantial yield loss if not controlled, especially during the first 30-45 days after sowing, which is the most critical weed-competition window. Weed management approaches include:
- Thorough pre-sowing field preparation to physically remove weed roots, rhizomes, and reduce the weed seed bank.
- Use of clean, weed-free certified seed to avoid introducing new weed seeds into the field.
- Crop rotation with different crop types (e.g., rotating with rice, cotton, or legumes) to break the life cycle of wheat-specific weeds such as Phalaris minor, which becomes harder to control under continuous wheat monocropping.
- Timely first irrigation combined with manual or mechanical weeding at around 30-35 days after sowing, since early weed removal has the largest impact on preserving yield.
- Herbicide application using selective herbicides appropriate for the specific weed spectrum present (grassy weed herbicides versus broadleaf herbicides, or combination products), applied at the correct crop and weed growth stage as recommended by local agricultural experts, to maximize efficacy and avoid crop injury.
- Herbicide resistance management, including rotating herbicide modes of action and integrating non-chemical control methods, is increasingly important in regions where weeds such as Phalaris minor have developed resistance to commonly used herbicides after repeated use.

MAJOR WHEAT DISEASES
1. Rusts (collectively the most economically damaging group of wheat diseases worldwide):
   - Stem (black) rust - caused by the fungus Puccinia graminis f. sp. tritici. Typically appears later in the season, producing elongated, reddish-brown to black, powdery pustules on stems, leaf sheaths, and leaves; can cause severe yield loss, including complete crop failure in susceptible varieties under favorable (warm, humid) conditions.
   - Leaf (brown/orange) rust - caused by Puccinia triticina (formerly Puccinia recondita f. sp. tritici). Forms small, circular to oval, orange-brown pustules mainly scattered on the upper leaf surface; the most widespread of the wheat rusts in many regions, causing chronic yield losses even where it does not reach epidemic levels.
   - Stripe (yellow) rust - caused by Puccinia striiformis f. sp. tritici. Appears as bright yellow pustules arranged in narrow stripes running along the leaf veins; favored by cool, humid weather and can spread very rapidly across large areas, causing major regional epidemics and yield losses when susceptible varieties dominate.
2. Smuts and bunts:
   - Flag smut - caused by Urocystis tritici (previously Urocystis agropyri). A soil- and seed-borne disease affecting leaves and stems, producing long black dusty streaks/sori that rupture to release spores; can kill the plant or severely stunt it, particularly damaging in regions with a history of repeated wheat monoculture.
   - Loose smut - caused by Ustilago tritici. An internally seed-borne disease that destroys the entire ear, replacing the grain with a black powdery mass of spores that is dispersed by wind, leaving only the bare rachis; spreads mainly through infected seed, making seed treatment and use of certified disease-free seed the primary control method.
   - Karnal bunt - caused by Tilletia indica. A seed- and soil-borne disease that partially converts individual grains into black spore masses, imparting a characteristic fishy odor to the grain; primarily a grain-quality and trade/marketability issue rather than a major yield-reducing disease, but it can lead to rejection of grain lots for milling or export.
3. Foliar fungal diseases:
   - Powdery mildew - caused by Blumeria graminis f. sp. tritici. Appears as white to grayish powdery fungal growth on leaf surfaces, leaf sheaths, and sometimes the ear; favored by dense crop canopies, high humidity, and excessive nitrogen application; reduces photosynthetic area and can lower yield if severe.
   - Septoria leaf blotch (Septoria tritici blotch and Stagonospora nodorum blotch) - causes irregular brown to tan lesions, often with small dark fungal fruiting bodies visible within the lesions, on leaves; reduces the green leaf area available for grain filling, and severe infection can significantly reduce yield and grain quality.
   - Tan spot - caused by Pyrenophora tritici-repentis, produces tan/brown lesions often with a yellow halo, particularly common where wheat is grown in continuous rotation with wheat residue left on the soil surface (favoring reduced/no-till systems without proper residue management).
4. Root and crown diseases: Various soil-borne fungi (such as Fusarium and Bipolaris species) can cause seedling blight, root rot, and crown rot, particularly under moisture stress, poor drainage, or continuous wheat cropping without rotation.
Management principles across wheat diseases: use of resistant or tolerant varieties as the first line of defense (since resistance can break down over time as new pathogen races emerge, periodic variety updates are important); seed treatment with an appropriate fungicide before sowing to control seed-borne diseases such as loose smut and Karnal bunt; avoiding excessive nitrogen application, which promotes dense, humid canopies favorable to fungal disease development; ensuring proper field drainage to reduce root and crown disease risk; practicing crop rotation to reduce the buildup of soil-borne pathogens and residue-borne diseases; regular field monitoring/scouting, especially during cool, humid weather that favors rust and powdery mildew development; and timely application of foliar fungicides when disease is detected early and conditions favor its spread, following locally recommended products, rates, and timing.

MAJOR WHEAT PESTS
1. Aphids (several species) - small, soft-bodied, sap-sucking insects that cluster on leaves, stems, and developing ears; they weaken the plant by removing plant sap, excrete honeydew that promotes sooty mold growth, reduce grain quality and yield when populations are high, and importantly can transmit viral diseases such as barley yellow dwarf virus.
2. Termites - soil-dwelling insects that can damage the root system and lower stem, especially in light, sandy soils and in drought-stressed crops; damaged plants dry out, show poor growth, and may lodge or die in patches, particularly visible as irregular yellowing/drying patches in the field.
3. Armyworms and cutworms - caterpillar (larval) stages of certain moths that feed on leaves and can cause significant defoliation, particularly damaging during the early seedling and vegetative stages; cutworms in particular can cut young seedlings off at the base.
4. Wheat stem sawfly and other stem-boring insects - larvae bore into and feed within the stem, weakening it structurally and causing lodging or stem breakage, particularly near harvest.
5. Wheat aphid-transmitted and other viral diseases indirectly linked to insect pest pressure, underscoring the importance of aphid control not just for direct feeding damage but for disease prevention.
6. Rodents (rats and other small mammals) - can cause significant losses both to the standing crop (by cutting stems and consuming grain) and to harvested/stored grain if storage structures are not rodent-proof.
7. Storage pests (post-harvest) - various beetles and moths (such as grain weevils and flour beetles) can infest stored wheat grain if it is not dried to a safe moisture level and stored in clean, well-sealed, pest-proof structures.
Management principles: regular field monitoring/scouting to detect pest buildup before it reaches damaging levels; timely sowing within the recommended window to avoid the crop's most vulnerable growth stages coinciding with peak pest activity periods; use of resistant or tolerant varieties where available; maintaining field hygiene by removing crop residues and alternate host weeds that can harbor pests between seasons; encouraging natural predators and beneficial insects where possible as part of integrated pest management; and applying recommended insecticides only when pest populations exceed locally established economic threshold levels, using the correct product, dose, and timing as advised by agricultural extension services, to avoid unnecessary cost, resistance development, and harm to beneficial insects.

LODGING: CAUSES AND MANAGEMENT
Lodging (the crop falling over or bending at the stem base) is a significant cause of yield loss and harvesting difficulty in wheat. Common causes include excessive nitrogen fertilizer application without a corresponding balance of potassium, which promotes tall, weak, and thin-walled stems; dense plant populations from an excessively high seed rate, which increases competition for light and produces thinner, weaker stems; use of tall, lodging-susceptible varieties, particularly under high-input, high-fertility conditions; strong winds or heavy rain, especially when combined with a saturated, waterlogged root zone that provides poor anchorage; and stem-boring insect damage or stem-based fungal disease that weakens the stem structurally. Management strategies include choosing shorter-strawed, lodging-resistant varieties where high inputs are used, applying nitrogen in appropriately timed and sized split doses rather than large single applications, ensuring adequate potassium supply, avoiding excessively high seed rates, and, in some systems, the use of plant growth regulators to reduce stem elongation and lodging risk under very high-fertility conditions.

CROP ROTATION AND CROPPING SYSTEMS
Wheat is commonly grown in rotation with a range of other crops depending on the region and irrigation availability, such as rice, cotton, maize, sugarcane, or various legume/pulse crops. Crop rotation offers multiple benefits: it helps break the life cycles of wheat-specific diseases and weeds (such as Phalaris minor and certain soil-borne pathogens) that build up under continuous wheat monoculture; rotating with legume crops can improve soil nitrogen status through biological nitrogen fixation, reducing fertilizer requirements for the following wheat crop; and diversifying crops spreads production and market risk for the farmer across the year. In some systems, wheat follows a summer crop such as rice or cotton in the same field within a single year, requiring careful timing of harvest of the preceding crop and land preparation to allow wheat to be sown within its optimum window.

MECHANIZATION IN WHEAT PRODUCTION
Modern wheat production increasingly relies on mechanization at multiple stages: tractor-drawn plows, disc harrows, and cultivators for land preparation; seed-cum-fertilizer drills for precise, uniform row sowing and simultaneous basal fertilizer placement; laser land levelers for precision leveling that improves irrigation efficiency and crop uniformity; sprayers for herbicide, fungicide, and foliar fertilizer application; and combine harvesters for efficient, timely harvesting and threshing, particularly important in regions where a narrow window exists between wheat harvest and the sowing of the following crop. Mechanization reduces labor requirements and turnaround time between crops, and precision equipment such as laser levelers and seed drills can measurably improve both input use efficiency and yield compared to traditional manual methods.

HARVESTING
Wheat is ready for harvest at physiological maturity, when the crop turns from green to golden yellow and the grains become firm and hard, having reached their maximum dry weight; at this point, further ripening mainly involves loss of moisture rather than any increase in yield. For combine harvesting, grain moisture content is typically allowed to drop to around 20-25% or lower before harvest to allow efficient threshing and reduce grain damage; for manual harvesting with sickles followed by stacking, drying, and threshing, timing is somewhat more flexible but still aims to avoid over-ripening. Harvesting too early results in shriveled, immature, lower-weight grain and increased moisture content that complicates storage; harvesting too late increases the risk of grain shattering and losses in the field due to wind, rain, lodging, or bird damage, as well as potential quality deterioration from weathering. Threshing separates grain from straw and chaff, either mechanically via combine harvester or threshing machine, or traditionally by treading/beating followed by winnowing.

POST-HARVEST HANDLING AND STORAGE
After harvesting and threshing, grain should be properly cleaned to remove chaff, weed seeds, and foreign material, and then dried to a safe moisture level, generally around 12% or below, before storage, since higher moisture content promotes mold growth, grain heating, and rapid insect pest buildup during storage. Grain should be stored in clean, dry, well-ventilated, and preferably pest-proof and rodent-proof storage structures, kept away from direct contact with damp floors or walls. Bags or bulk storage containers should be inspected periodically for signs of moisture ingress, insect infestation, or rodent activity, and grain should be aerated or turned periodically in bulk storage systems if facilities allow, to prevent moisture and temperature buildup. Proper post-harvest handling directly affects both the quantity of grain preserved and its quality and market value at the time of sale.

GRAIN QUALITY PARAMETERS
Wheat grain quality is assessed through several parameters relevant to milling, baking, and market value, including test weight (bulk density, an indicator of grain soundness and milling yield), grain protein content (important for bread-making strength; higher protein is generally desired for bread wheat while lower protein may be preferred for some pastry or biscuit applications), gluten content and strength (determines dough elasticity and bread-making performance), moisture content (affects both storability and, in many markets, the price received), and freedom from foreign material, shriveled or damaged grains, and disease-affected grain such as Karnal bunt-infected kernels. Grain quality is influenced by variety, growing conditions (particularly temperature and moisture stress during grain filling), nitrogen nutrition (higher nitrogen availability generally increases grain protein content, within limits), and post-harvest handling and storage conditions.

ECONOMIC AND NUTRITIONAL IMPORTANCE
Wheat is a major source of dietary energy, protein, and B-vitamins for a large portion of the world's population, particularly where it forms the base of staple foods such as bread and flatbreads. Economically, wheat is a critical crop for both smallholder food security and large-scale commercial agriculture, and it is one of the most widely traded agricultural commodities globally. Wheat straw, a by-product of grain production, has additional value as livestock fodder, bedding material, and in some regions as a raw material for other industrial uses, adding to the overall economic value of the crop beyond grain alone.
