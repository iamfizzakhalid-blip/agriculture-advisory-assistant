Sugarcane, scientifically belonging to the genus Saccharum, most commonly Saccharum officinarum and its hybrids, is a tall, perennial grass grown primarily for the sugar stored in its thick stalks, though it is also used for biofuel, fodder, and various byproducts. Unlike maize and rice, which are annual crops grown from seed each season and harvested once, sugarcane is a semi-perennial crop, meaning a single planting can be harvested multiple times over several years through a process called ratooning, which will be explained in detail further down. Sugarcane is also a C4 plant like maize, meaning it photosynthesizes very efficiently in hot, sunny conditions, and this is part of why it can accumulate such large amounts of sugar in its stalk over a long growing season. The plant consists of a clump of thick, jointed stalks called canes that can grow anywhere from about two to six meters tall depending on variety and growing conditions, with each stalk divided into nodes and internodes; the nodes are the slightly swollen joints where a bud, a leaf, and root primordia are located, and the internodes are the sections between nodes where sugar accumulates in the form of sucrose stored in the stalk tissue. The leaves are long, blade-like, and arranged alternately along the stalk, wrapping around it at the base with a sheath, and as the plant grows and lower leaves age and dry out, they are usually stripped or naturally fall away, a process farmers refer to as detrashing when done manually. Sugarcane does produce a flower, called an arrow or tassel, at the top of mature canes under certain daylength and climate conditions, but commercial sugarcane is not grown from the true seed produced by this flower, because seed-grown plants are highly variable and slow to establish; instead, sugarcane is propagated vegetatively from stem cuttings.

The life cycle of a single sugarcane crop cycle, called plant cane, generally spans somewhere between ten and eighteen months depending on the variety and climate, which is considerably longer than the growing season of annual crops like maize, wheat, or rice. This long cycle is generally divided into four broad phases. The germination phase covers the first several weeks after planting, during which buds on the planted stem cuttings sprout and send up the first shoots, and this stage is quite sensitive to soil moisture and temperature, since the buds need adequate warmth and moisture to sprout reliably. The tillering phase follows, during which the plant sends up multiple additional shoots from its base, much like tillering in rice, and the number of productive tillers that survive to become harvestable stalks strongly influences the eventual yield of cane per unit area. After tillering slows down, the crop enters the grand growth phase, sometimes called the stalk elongation phase, which is the longest phase of the cycle and the period during which the plant grows most rapidly in height and thickness, building up the bulk of the biomass that will later store sugar; this phase demands the most water and nutrients of any stage in the crop's life. Finally, the crop enters the maturation and ripening phase, during which vertical growth slows down substantially and the plant redirects its energy toward converting and storing sucrose in the stalk rather than building new tissue, and this is the period farmers watch most closely to determine when sugar content, referred to in the industry as the crop's brix or sucrose percentage, has reached its peak and the cane is ready to harvest.

Sugarcane needs a genuinely hot climate and a long frost-free growing season to perform well, generally thriving with average temperatures somewhere between about twenty and thirty five degrees Celsius during active growth, though the ideal temperature during the final ripening phase is often somewhat cooler, since a period of cooler nights and reduced rainfall near maturity actually helps encourage the plant to accumulate more sugar rather than continuing vegetative growth. Sugarcane is highly frost sensitive and even a light frost can damage or kill the exposed parts of the plant and reduce sugar quality in the stalk, which is why it is mostly restricted to tropical and subtropical regions or warmer temperate zones with a sufficiently long frost-free window. Because of its long growing cycle and large biomass, sugarcane has a very high total water requirement compared to most annual crops, typically needing somewhere in the range of fifteen hundred to twenty five hundred millimeters of water across the full crop cycle when grown under irrigation, though this varies enormously by climate, soil, and crop duration; water demand is highest during the grand growth phase, while a deliberate reduction in water toward the end of the season, sometimes called moisture stress or drying off, is actually used deliberately by farmers to help trigger and improve the ripening and sugar accumulation process. Sugarcane requires full sunlight for good growth and sugar accumulation and performs poorly in shaded conditions.

In terms of soil, sugarcane grows best in deep, well drained, fertile loamy soils, though it is a relatively adaptable crop compared to some others and can be grown across a fairly wide range of soil types, from sandy loams to heavier clay soils, provided drainage is adequate, since sugarcane does not tolerate prolonged waterlogging any better than most other crops and root damage from standing water can significantly reduce yield. Ideal soil pH for sugarcane is roughly six to seven point five, close to neutral, though the crop shows some tolerance for mildly acidic or mildly alkaline soils outside this range. Because sugarcane occupies the same field for a long period, often well over a year for the plant crop alone and then additional years if ratooned, good initial soil preparation and fertility management matter a great deal, since there are fewer natural opportunities to correct soil problems partway through the cycle compared to a short-duration annual crop.

Sugarcane varieties are generally selected based on several key criteria that matter enormously to farmers and to the sugar industry. Cane yield, meaning the total tonnage of harvestable stalks produced per unit area, is one major factor, but just as important is sucrose content, often expressed as a commercial cane sugar percentage or CCS, since two varieties producing the same tonnage of cane can differ substantially in how much actual sugar can be extracted from that cane. Maturity duration also matters, with early maturing varieties ripening in a shorter window and late maturing varieties needing a longer season to reach peak sugar content, and farmers often plant a mix of early, mid, and late maturing varieties to spread out their harvest and keep the sugar mill supplied over a longer crushing season rather than having the entire crop ready at once. Ratooning ability, meaning how well a variety regrows and performs after being cut for a first harvest, is another major consideration, since varieties with strong ratooning ability allow farmers to get several profitable harvests from a single planting before yields decline enough to require replanting. Disease resistance, particularly resistance to major diseases like red rot and smut discussed further below, along with tolerance to lodging, drought, and cold, are all also factored into variety selection depending on local growing conditions.

Land preparation for sugarcane typically involves deep plowing to break up compacted soil and allow good root penetration, since the crop will occupy the field for a long period and benefits from a deep, well-loosened root zone, followed by harrowing to break clods and level the field. Because sugarcane is usually planted in furrows rather than broadcast or drilled like a cereal crop, the prepared field is typically laid out with furrows spaced somewhere around seventy five to one hundred twenty centimeters apart, with the exact spacing depending on variety, local practice, and whether the farmer plans to use machinery for later operations like inter-row cultivation or mechanical harvesting. Incorporating farmyard manure or compost into the soil before planting, similar to practice in maize and rice, helps build organic matter and supports the crop's fertility needs over its long growing cycle.

Planting sugarcane is quite different from planting maize, rice, or wheat, because it is not grown from true seed in commercial practice but rather from vegetative cuttings called setts, which are sections of mature, healthy cane stalk, typically containing two or three buds each, cut from a specially maintained seed cane crop that is kept relatively young and disease-free specifically to supply planting material. These setts are laid horizontally end to end or slightly overlapping in the bottom of the prepared furrows and then covered with a shallow layer of soil, after which the buds on each sett sprout upward to establish the new crop, essentially the same way a potato grows from a planted tuber piece rather than from true botanical seed. The amount of seed cane needed per unit area is fairly substantial compared to the seed rates of cereal crops, since whole cane stalks rather than small seeds are being used, and farmers typically need a meaningful portion of their own harvest, or a dedicated seed cane plot, set aside each year to provide planting material for the next crop cycle if they intend to replant rather than ratoon. Before planting, setts are sometimes treated by dipping them briefly in hot water or a fungicide solution to kill pathogens that might be present on the surface of the cane and reduce the risk of disease being carried into the new crop through the planting material itself. Planting time is generally chosen so that the germination and early tillering phases coincide with warm temperatures and adequate moisture, and specific planting windows vary a great deal by region and climate.

One of the most distinctive aspects of sugarcane farming, and one that directly relates to a common point of confusion for farmers, is the difference between plant cane and ratoon cane. Plant cane refers to the crop grown from freshly planted setts, as just described, representing the first harvest cycle from a new planting. After this plant cane crop is harvested, meaning the stalks are cut down close to ground level, the root system and the base of the stalks, called stubble, remain alive in the soil, and if managed properly, this stubble will sprout new shoots on its own without any need to replant, producing a second crop known as the first ratoon. This ratoon crop can then itself be harvested and allowed to regrow again into a second ratoon, and this process can sometimes be repeated for several cycles, known as ratoon after ratoon, before yields decline enough that the field is eventually plowed out and replanted with fresh setts to restart the cycle. Ratooning is attractive to farmers because it avoids the substantial cost and labor of land preparation and replanting for each cycle, but ratoon crops generally yield somewhat less than the original plant cane crop, and this yield decline tends to become more pronounced with each successive ratoon, partly due to a gradual buildup of pests, diseases, and soil-borne problems in a field that is not disturbed by plowing between cycles, and partly due to gradual depletion of the stubble's vigor over repeated cutting cycles. Because of this, most farmers plan to ratoon a field for a limited number of cycles, commonly somewhere around two to four ratoons depending on variety, management, and how well yields are holding up, before finally replanting with fresh setts to reset the cycle and restore full yield potential.

Nutrient management in sugarcane must account for the crop's very large biomass and long growing cycle, which means it removes substantially more total nutrients from the soil over its cycle than most annual crops, even though nutrient application is often expressed per year to allow comparison. Nitrogen is again the most critical nutrient, with typical total applications for a full plant cane cycle often falling somewhere in the range of150 to 250 kilograms per hectare depending on region, variety, and yield target, generally split into several doses applied at planting, during early tillering, and again during the early part of the grand growth phase, since heavy nitrogen application too late in the season can actually delay ripening and reduce sugar accumulation by keeping the plant in a vegetative rather than sugar-storing mode. Nitrogen deficiency in sugarcane shows up as an overall pale yellowish green color, particularly in older leaves, along with reduced tillering and stunted growth, similar in principle to nitrogen deficiency symptoms in other cereals. Phosphorus supports strong early root and tiller development and is typically applied as a basal dose at planting time, since it does not move readily through the soil profile. Potassium is especially important in sugarcane because it directly supports sugar transport and accumulation within the stalk in addition to its usual roles in stalk strength and disease resistance, so potassium deficiency can specifically hurt sugar content even when cane tonnage looks reasonably good; deficiency symptoms typically appear as yellowing and scorching along the margins of older leaves. Sugarcane also has a relatively high demand for micronutrients over its long cycle, with iron, zinc, and manganese deficiencies occurring in some soils, often showing as pale interveinal yellowing or streaking on younger leaves, and these are generally corrected with targeted micronutrient fertilizer applications, either to the soil or as foliar sprays, once identified.

Irrigation and water management in sugarcane must account for the crop's long duration and high total water need. Water demand is relatively modest during germination and early tillering, rises substantially through the grand growth phase, when the crop is building most of its biomass and is most sensitive to water shortage, and is then deliberately reduced in the weeks before harvest as part of the ripening process, since allowing the crop to experience mild water stress near maturity encourages the plant to convert more of its stored resources into sucrose rather than continuing active vegetative growth, generally improving the sugar content of the harvested cane. Excessive irrigation right up to harvest, by contrast, tends to keep the crop growing vegetatively and can reduce the sugar percentage even if total cane tonnage looks high. As with other crops, waterlogging is harmful, and sugarcane fields need adequate drainage to prevent standing water from damaging the root system, particularly during the earlier stages of the crop cycle.

Weed control is especially important early in the sugarcane cycle, roughly during the germination and tillering phases, before the crop's canopy has closed enough to shade out competing weeds on its own; this critical weed-free period can extend longer in sugarcane than in shorter duration crops like maize, sometimes lasting several months given how slowly the young cane crop initially develops compared to its eventual size. Weed management methods include pre-emergence and post-emergence herbicides suited to the local weed spectrum, inter-row cultivation, and mulching with crop residue or dried leaves stripped from the cane itself, sometimes called trash mulching, which both suppresses weeds and helps conserve soil moisture.

A number of pests affect sugarcane. The sugarcane stem borer, or various species of stalk borer depending on region, is one of the most damaging pests, with larvae tunneling into the stalk and causing dead heart in young shoots, similar to the stem borer damage seen in maize and rice, along with internal tunneling in older canes that reduces both cane weight and sugar content and can create entry points for secondary rot infections. The sugarcane top borer and internode borer cause similar tunneling damage at different parts of the plant. White grubs, the larval stage of certain beetles, feed on roots underground and can cause significant stand loss, particularly in sandy soils. Sugarcane woolly aphid is a sap-sucking pest that can build up heavily on the underside of leaves, weakening the plant and reducing sugar content, while also producing a sticky honeydew secretion that promotes sooty mold growth on the leaves. Termites can also damage both planted setts before germination and the base of growing stalks. As with other crops, regular field monitoring, resistant varieties where available, conservation of natural predators, and targeted rather than routine pesticide application are generally recommended over blanket spraying.

Diseases are a serious and sometimes crop-threatening concern in sugarcane, made more significant by the fact that the crop is vegetatively propagated, meaning diseases present in planting material, the setts themselves, can be carried directly into a new crop rather than needing to be reintroduced fresh each season the way many seed-borne diseases work in annual crops. Red rot, caused by a fungus, is one of the most economically devastating sugarcane diseases in many regions, causing a reddish discoloration with characteristic white cross-bands visible when an infected stalk is split open lengthwise, along with a sour smell, wilting, and drying of leaves on affected stalks; this is a very important disease for a farmer to be able to recognize, because reddish leaf symptoms or a general wilting, dying appearance in sugarcane are quite often linked to red rot rather than a simple nutrient issue, and confirming the cause typically requires actually splitting open a suspect stalk to check for the internal reddening and cross-banding pattern, since surface leaf symptoms alone can be easy to confuse with drought stress or nutrient deficiency. Red rot spreads readily through infected setts used as planting material, which is one of the major reasons hot water or fungicide treatment of setts before planting, along with sourcing seed cane from disease-free nurseries, is so strongly recommended. Smut is another major disease, easily recognized by a long, black, whip-like structure that emerges from the growing point of an infected stalk in place of a normal shoot, and severely reduces both yield and the suitability of affected stalks for use as future seed cane. Ratoon stunting disease is a bacterial disease that does not always show obvious external symptoms but can cause a significant, sometimes hard-to-diagnose reduction in cane yield and vigor, particularly in ratoon crops, and is also spread mainly through infected setts and contaminated cutting tools, making sett treatment and tool sanitation important preventive measures. Various leaf diseases, including several fungal leaf spots and rusts, can also reduce photosynthetic leaf area, though they are generally less destructive than red rot, smut, or ratoon stunting disease. Because so many of the most serious sugarcane diseases spread through infected planting material, using certified, disease-free seed cane, treating setts before planting, and avoiding the use of visibly diseased stalks as seed cane are among the most important disease prevention practices in the crop.

Harvesting sugarcane is timed around peak sucrose accumulation rather than a single fixed physiological maturity marker like the black layer stage in maize, and farmers and mills typically judge readiness using a combination of visual cues, such as a change in stalk color, drying and yellowing of the lower leaves, and reduced vertical growth, along with actual sugar content testing using instruments that measure brix, meaning the concentration of dissolved solids largely made up of sucrose in the juice squeezed from a sample stalk; cane is generally harvested once this reading indicates that sucrose content has reached its economically optimal peak, since harvesting too early sacrifices potential sugar yield and harvesting too late can allow sugar content to decline again as the plant's stored sucrose begins to be used for other purposes or lost through natural processes late in the season. Harvesting itself involves cutting the stalks close to ground level, traditionally done by hand with a machete-like tool, which remains common in many smallholder and labor-abundant regions, or increasingly by mechanical harvesters in larger, more mechanized operations, which cut, strip leaves, and chop the cane into shorter billets in a single pass. Timing of harvest relative to ratooning is also important, since cutting the stalks cleanly and close to the ground, without excessively damaging the stubble left behind, directly affects how well the field will regrow as a ratoon crop afterward.

Unlike grain crops such as maize, rice, or wheat, sugarcane has essentially no long-term on-farm storage step, because the sugar content of harvested cane begins to decline fairly quickly once the stalks are cut, due to ongoing respiration and the activity of naturally occurring microorganisms that begin breaking down sucrose in the cut stalk; because of this, harvested cane needs to be transported and processed at a sugar mill as quickly as possible after cutting, ideally within about twenty four to forty eight hours, to avoid meaningful losses in recoverable sugar, which is quite different from crops like maize or rice where proper drying allows the grain to be stored safely for months. At the mill, the harvested cane is crushed to extract the juice, which is then clarified, boiled down, and crystallized through a multi-step industrial process to produce raw and eventually refined sugar, with the fibrous leftover material after juice extraction, called bagasse, commonly burned as fuel to power the mill itself or used to make paper products, and molasses, a thick byproduct syrup left after as much sugar as practical has been crystallized out, used in products ranging from animal feed to the production of ethanol and rum.

Beyond raw and refined sugar, sugarcane has a number of other significant uses. It is a major feedstock for ethanol production in some countries, notably as a large-scale biofuel source, where cane juice or molasses is fermented and distilled into ethanol used either as a fuel additive or, in some countries, as the dominant transport fuel in a blended or standalone form. Bagasse, besides being burned as mill fuel, is also used in the manufacture of paper, particleboard, and other fiber-based products. The tops and leaves of the cane plant, along with some of the trash generated during harvest, are commonly used as livestock fodder in many farming regions. Jaggery and other traditional unrefined or minimally processed sugar products, made by boiling down cane juice without the full industrial refining process used for white sugar, remain widely produced and consumed in many parts of the world, particularly in South Asia.

From a farming and economic perspective, sugarcane requires a considerably larger upfront investment and a much longer time to first return than most annual crops, since the plant crop cycle alone can take over a year before the first harvest, and the substantial cost of seed cane, land preparation, and the labor involved in planting setts by hand all represent significant early expenses. However, because a single planting can then be ratooned for several additional harvests without needing to be replanted each time, the effective cost per harvest tends to average down over the full multi-year cycle of a plant crop followed by its ratoons, which is a major part of sugarcane's economic appeal compared to crops that must be fully replanted every single season. Yield potential varies enormously with climate, variety, and management, with cane tonnage commonly ranging from as low as thirty to forty tons per hectare in less favorable rainfed conditions with older varieties, up to well over one hundred tons per hectare in well managed, irrigated systems using modern high-yielding varieties. Because sugarcane is almost always grown under contract with, or in close coordination with, a specific sugar mill that will process the harvested cane, farmers typically need to plan their planting and harvest timing around the mill's crushing season and capacity, and payment is often based not just on the tonnage of cane delivered but also on its measured sugar content, meaning that management practices that boost sucrose percentage, such as appropriate late-season water reduction and balanced potassium nutrition, directly affect a farmer's income in a way that is somewhat more direct than in most grain crops, where payment is typically based on weight and basic quality grading alone rather than a specific internal chemical content measurement.
