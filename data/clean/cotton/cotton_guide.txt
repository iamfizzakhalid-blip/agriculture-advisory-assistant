Cotton Cultivation Complete Guide: Botany, Varieties, Sowing, Fertilizer, Irrigation, Diseases, Pests, Growth Stages, Picking, and Yield Improvement

INTRODUCTION TO COTTON
Cotton (Gossypium species, mainly Gossypium hirsutum - American upland cotton, and Gossypium arboreum/Gossypium herbaceum - desi/Asiatic cotton) is one of the world's most important fiber crops and also a significant oilseed crop, since cottonseed yields edible oil and protein-rich cake used as livestock feed. Cotton belongs to the family Malvaceae. It is grown mainly as a kharif (summer) crop in South Asia, sown in spring/early summer and harvested from autumn into winter, with picking often continuing over several months as bolls mature progressively. Cotton fiber (lint) is the raw material for the textile industry, while cottonseed is processed for oil and animal feed, and cotton stalks/residue can be used as fuel or organic matter after harvest.

BOTANICAL CHARACTERISTICS
Cotton is a perennial shrub grown as an annual crop in commercial cultivation. It has a deep, well-developed taproot system with lateral roots, allowing it to draw moisture and nutrients from a considerable soil depth, which contributes to its relative drought tolerance compared to many other crops once established. The stem is woody at the base and branches into vegetative (monopodial) branches lower on the plant and fruiting (sympodial) branches higher up, which bear the flowers and bolls. Cotton is largely self-pollinated, though some cross-pollination by insects (especially bees) occurs. The flower is large and showy, typically opening cream/yellow and turning pink to red before wilting within about a day of opening. After fertilization, the flower develops into a boll (seed capsule) containing several locules, each with multiple seeds; the seed surface is covered with the two types of hairs that make up cotton fiber - long fibers (lint), which are spun into yarn, and short fibers (fuzz/linters), which remain attached to the seed and are used in industrial products such as cellulose derivatives. As the boll matures and dries, it splits open (dehisces) and the fluffy white or cream-colored lint becomes visible and ready for picking.

TYPES AND CLASSIFICATION OF COTTON
- Upland cotton (Gossypium hirsutum): The dominant commercial type grown worldwide, accounting for the vast majority of global cotton production; medium to long staple fiber suitable for most textile uses.
- Desi/Asiatic cotton (Gossypium arboreum and Gossypium herbaceum): Traditional types grown mainly in parts of South Asia, generally more hardy and tolerant to adverse conditions but producing coarser, shorter fiber and lower yields than upland cotton.
- Extra-long staple/Egyptian and Pima cotton (Gossypium barbadense): Produces very long, fine, high-value fiber used for premium textiles, but generally requires more specific climatic conditions and management.
- Bt cotton: Genetically modified varieties incorporating genes from the soil bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis, which confer resistance to certain bollworm pests, reducing the need for insecticide sprays against these specific pests; widely adopted in many cotton-growing countries, though effectiveness against non-target pests (such as sucking insects) is limited, and resistance management practices are needed to maintain efficacy over time.

CLIMATE AND SOIL REQUIREMENTS
Cotton is a warm-season crop requiring a long, frost-free growing period with plenty of sunshine. Optimum temperature for germination is around 18-30°C, with growth being favored by warm days (around 25-35°C) and moderately warm nights; temperatures below about 15°C slow growth and germination considerably, and frost damages or kills the crop, making cotton highly sensitive to cold at both the start and end of the season. Cotton requires a relatively long frost-free period, generally around 180-200 days from sowing to final picking depending on variety and region. While cotton has moderate drought tolerance once its deep root system is established, adequate moisture is essential during flowering and boll development for good yield; conversely, excessive rainfall or high humidity during boll opening and picking promotes boll rot and lint quality deterioration, and waterlogging at any stage severely damages the crop.
Cotton grows best on deep, well-drained soils with good water-holding capacity, such as medium to heavy loams and clay loams; black cotton soils (vertisols), rich in clay and known for their moisture retention, are traditionally associated with cotton cultivation in several regions. Soil pH between about 6.0 and 8.0 is generally suitable, and cotton has moderate tolerance to soil salinity compared to many other crops, though yield and quality decline under high salinity. Poorly drained or waterlogged soils are unsuitable, as cotton is highly sensitive to root zone waterlogging, which causes root damage, shedding of squares (flower buds) and bolls, and stunted growth.

LAND PREPARATION
Good land preparation supports deep root penetration and proper moisture management, both important for cotton. Typical steps include:
1. Deep plowing, especially important for cotton given its deep taproot system, to break any hardpan and allow roots to penetrate deeply for moisture and nutrient access.
2. Subsequent harrowing/cultivation to break clods and produce a fine, workable seedbed.
3. Leveling the field to ensure uniform irrigation water distribution and to avoid waterlogging in low spots, which cotton is particularly sensitive to.
4. Incorporating farmyard manure or compost before final land preparation to improve soil structure, organic matter, and moisture-holding capacity, particularly valuable in medium to heavy soils prone to compaction.
5. Removing residues of the previous crop and controlling perennial weeds before sowing.
6. In regions using ridge-and-furrow or bed planting systems, forming ridges/beds at this stage to improve drainage and facilitate irrigation management through the season.

COTTON VARIETIES AND SEED SELECTION
Choice of variety significantly affects yield, fiber quality, pest/disease resistance, and management requirements. Key factors in variety selection include:
- Maturity duration (early, medium, or late maturing), which affects the length of the growing season needed and suitability for the local frost-free window and any subsequent crop in rotation.
- Yield potential under local management, irrigation, and soil conditions.
- Fiber quality traits relevant to market value, including staple length, fiber strength, fineness (micronaire), and uniformity.
- Resistance or tolerance to major pests, particularly bollworms (where Bt varieties are used) and sucking pests such as whitefly and jassids.
- Resistance or tolerance to major diseases prevalent in the region, such as cotton leaf curl virus in areas where it is endemic, and bacterial blight.
- Growth habit and plant architecture (compact vs. spreading), which affects plant population recommendations and suitability for mechanical operations.
- Response to irrigation and fertilizer inputs.
Farmers should select varieties recommended by local agricultural research institutes for their specific region, since disease and pest pressures (particularly viral diseases spread by insect vectors) vary significantly by location and change over time as new pest biotypes or disease strains emerge. Certified seed from a reliable source is recommended over farm-saved seed, both for genetic purity and, in the case of Bt cotton, to ensure the seed actually carries the intended trait and appropriate refuge seed requirements (where mandated) are met to slow the development of pest resistance to the Bt trait.

SOWING TIME
Cotton is generally sown in spring to early summer once soil and air temperatures are reliably warm enough for good germination and there is no further risk of frost, with exact timing varying considerably by region and local climate. Sowing when soil temperature is too low results in poor, slow, and uneven germination and increases seedling vulnerability to soil-borne diseases and pests. Sowing too late shortens the effective growing season, delaying flowering and boll development into periods of less favorable temperature or approaching the end of the frost-free window, which can reduce yield and delay or compromise the harvest of later-formed bolls. Local agricultural extension recommendations should be followed for the optimal sowing window, as it is tightly linked to local temperature patterns and the risk of late spring frosts or early autumn frosts.

SOWING METHOD, PLANT POPULATION, AND SEED RATE
Cotton is typically sown in rows, either on flat ground or on ridges/beds, using a seed drill, planter, or manual dibbling depending on farm scale and mechanization level.
- Row spacing varies by variety growth habit, region, and management system, but commonly falls in a range of roughly 60-100 cm between rows, with plant-to-plant spacing within the row adjusted to achieve the recommended plant population for the variety and region.
- Optimum plant population balances yield potential against competition for light, water, and nutrients; both excessively low and excessively high plant populations can reduce yield - too low wastes land and light interception potential, while too high increases competition, vegetative growth at the expense of boll development, and disease/pest pressure due to a denser, more humid canopy.
- Seed rate depends on seed size, germination percentage, spacing, and whether seed is delinted (fuzz removed) or fuzzy; delinted seed flows better through precision planters and is commonly used with mechanical planting.
- Sowing depth is typically around 3-5 cm, adjusted for soil moisture and texture - slightly shallower in heavier, moisture-retentive soils and slightly deeper in lighter, drier soils to reach adequate moisture.
- Seed treatment with an appropriate fungicide and/or insecticide before sowing helps protect germinating seedlings from soil-borne diseases (such as seedling blight/damping-off) and early-season sucking pests, and is a widely recommended practice.
- Thinning of excess seedlings after emergence (where more than one seed per station is sown, or after broadcasting) helps establish the target plant population and reduces early competition among seedlings.

CROP GROWTH STAGES
Understanding cotton's growth stages helps with timing of irrigation, fertilizer, and pest/disease management:
1. Germination and emergence: Seed absorbs moisture and the seedling emerges above the soil surface; a critical and vulnerable stage sensitive to soil crusting, cold soil, and seedling pests/diseases.
2. Seedling establishment: Development of the first true leaves and initial root system expansion.
3. Vegetative growth (squaring initiation): The plant develops main-stem leaves and begins forming vegetative and fruiting branches; the first flower buds (squares) appear on fruiting branches.
4. Squaring: Flower buds (squares) continue to develop and increase in number; this stage is particularly vulnerable to sucking pests and early bollworm attack, since damage to squares directly reduces potential boll (and yield) numbers.
5. Flowering: Squares open into flowers, which are pollinated (mostly self-pollination, some insect-assisted) and then wilt within about a day, with the base of the flower developing into a young boll.
6. Boll development: Fertilized ovaries develop into bolls that enlarge and mature over several weeks; this is a critical period for water and nutrient supply, as stress here directly reduces boll size, seed cotton weight, and fiber quality.
7. Boll maturation and opening (boll burst): Mature bolls dry down and split open, exposing the fluffy lint, which is the point at which picking begins for that boll; because flowering and boll set occur over an extended period on a single plant, picking is typically done in multiple rounds over several weeks to months as different bolls mature.
8. End of season/defoliation (where practiced): In some systems, particularly where machine picking is used, a defoliant may be applied late in the season to remove remaining leaves, promoting more uniform boll opening and cleaner mechanical harvesting.

IRRIGATION REQUIREMENTS AND SCHEDULING
Cotton has a relatively deep root system and moderate drought tolerance compared to many field crops, but water stress at key growth stages significantly affects yield and fiber quality. Key considerations:
- Establishment stage: Adequate soil moisture is needed at sowing and during germination/emergence for uniform stand establishment; both drought stress and waterlogging at this stage are damaging.
- Vegetative and squaring stages: Moderate, well-managed irrigation supports healthy vegetative growth and square formation without promoting excessive vegetative growth at the expense of reproductive development; over-irrigation combined with high nitrogen at this stage can cause excessive vegetative growth ("rank growth") and delayed maturity.
- Flowering and boll development: This is generally the most water-sensitive period for cotton; adequate, consistent moisture supply during flowering and boll filling is critical for maximizing boll retention, boll size, and fiber quality, and water stress during this window typically causes the greatest yield loss, including shedding of squares, flowers, and young bolls.
- Boll maturation/opening stage: Irrigation is typically reduced or stopped as bolls mature and begin opening, since excess moisture at this stage can promote boll rot, poor fiber quality, and delayed, uneven boll opening; the final irrigation is usually timed to allow the field to dry sufficiently for picking.
Cotton is highly sensitive to waterlogging at any growth stage, since standing water reduces root zone oxygen and quickly leads to root damage, shedding of squares and bolls, and stunted growth; ensuring good field drainage and avoiding over-irrigation is as important as ensuring adequate water supply. The exact number and timing of irrigations depends heavily on soil type, rainfall pattern, and irrigation method (flood, furrow, sprinkler, or drip), with drip irrigation generally offering the most efficient water use and the ability to combine irrigation with fertilizer application (fertigation).

FERTILIZER AND NUTRIENT MANAGEMENT
Cotton requires a balanced supply of nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and, on many soils, micronutrients such as zinc and boron, for healthy vegetative growth balanced with strong reproductive (square, flower, and boll) development.
- Nitrogen: Supports vegetative growth, leaf area, and overall plant vigor; however, excessive nitrogen, particularly late in the season, promotes excessive vegetative ("rank") growth at the expense of square and boll retention, delays maturity, and increases susceptibility to certain pests and diseases due to a dense, humid canopy. Nitrogen is generally applied in split doses, with a portion at or soon after sowing and the remainder during the vegetative-to-squaring and flowering stages, timed to support reproductive development without excessive late-season vegetative growth.
- Phosphorus: Supports root development, energy transfer, and early plant establishment; because phosphorus is relatively immobile in soil, it is generally applied as a basal dose at or before sowing.
- Potassium: Plays an important role in boll development, fiber quality, and the plant's ability to withstand stress; adequate potassium supply supports proper boll filling and reduces the incidence of certain physiological disorders sometimes associated with potassium deficiency in high-yielding cotton crops; typically applied as a basal dose, though it may be split on soils with lower potassium-supplying capacity.
- Zinc and boron: Micronutrient deficiencies, particularly zinc and boron, are relatively common in cotton-growing soils and can significantly affect square and boll retention; boron in particular plays a specific role in flower and boll development, and deficiency can cause excessive shedding of squares and bolls even when other nutrients are adequate; these are usually corrected through soil or foliar applications based on soil test results or observed deficiency symptoms.
- Farmyard manure and organic amendments applied during land preparation improve soil structure and moisture retention, which is particularly valuable in medium to heavy cotton-growing soils prone to compaction, complementing rather than replacing balanced chemical fertilization in high-yield systems.
A soil test before the season is recommended, since nutrient requirements vary with soil fertility, texture, and cropping history; over-application of nitrogen in particular is a common cause of yield and quality problems in cotton due to its strong effect on vegetative-versus-reproductive growth balance.

WEED MANAGEMENT
Cotton is a relatively poor early-season competitor with weeds due to its slower initial growth compared to many weed species, making early weed control particularly important. Common weeds vary by region but often include various grasses and broadleaf species. Weed management practices include:
- Thorough pre-sowing land preparation to reduce the existing weed seed bank and remove perennial weed roots/rhizomes.
- Timely and repeated interculture (hoeing/cultivation between rows) during the early vegetative period, since the first 45-60 days after sowing are generally the most critical period for weed competition in cotton.
- Herbicide application, including pre-emergence herbicides applied at or shortly after sowing to control early-germinating weeds, and post-emergence herbicides where appropriate and compatible with the crop stage and, where relevant, any herbicide-tolerance trait in the variety being grown.
- Mulching (organic or plastic) in some systems to suppress weed growth and conserve soil moisture, particularly in smaller-scale or high-value production systems.
- Maintaining clean field borders and irrigation channels to reduce weed seed spread into the field.
Weeds compete with cotton for light, water, and nutrients, and can also serve as alternate hosts for certain cotton pests and diseases, making weed control relevant to overall pest and disease management as well as direct yield protection.

MAJOR COTTON DISEASES
1. Cotton leaf curl virus (CLCuV): A viral disease transmitted by the whitefly (Bemisia tabaci), causing upward or downward curling of leaves, thickening and vein swelling (enations) on the underside of leaves, stunted growth, and significant yield loss where the disease and its vector are prevalent; management relies heavily on using resistant/tolerant varieties, controlling the whitefly vector population, and removing/destroying severely infected plants to reduce virus spread.
2. Bacterial blight (angular leaf spot) - caused by Xanthomonas citri pv. malvacearum (formerly Xanthomonas campestris pv. malvacearum). Produces small, angular, water-soaked lesions on leaves that turn brown/black, can spread to stems and bolls causing boll rot and reduced fiber quality, and is favored by warm, humid, rainy conditions; seed-borne and residue-borne, so it is managed through resistant varieties, use of disease-free treated seed, and crop rotation/residue management.
3. Fusarium wilt - caused by Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. vasinfectum, a soil-borne fungus that invades the vascular system, causing yellowing, wilting, and often death of infected plants, particularly under conditions of nematode co-infection or plant stress; management relies mainly on resistant varieties and crop rotation, since the fungus can persist in soil for extended periods.
4. Verticillium wilt - caused by Verticillium dahliae, another soil-borne vascular wilt fungus causing leaf yellowing, wilting, and defoliation, generally favored by cooler soil temperatures than Fusarium wilt; managed through resistant varieties and crop rotation.
5. Seedling diseases (damping-off/seedling blight) - caused by various soil-borne fungi (such as Rhizoctonia, Pythium, and Fusarium species) affecting germinating seed and young seedlings, particularly under cool, wet soil conditions; managed through seed treatment with fungicide, avoiding sowing into excessively cold or wet soil, and ensuring good field drainage.
6. Boll rots - caused by various fungal and bacterial organisms that infect developing or maturing bolls, often entering through insect feeding wounds or in conditions of high humidity and rainfall during boll development and opening; management includes insect pest control (to reduce entry wounds), balanced nitrogen management (avoiding excessive vegetative growth that increases canopy humidity), and adequate plant spacing for airflow.
Management principles across cotton diseases: use of resistant or tolerant varieties suited to locally prevalent diseases, especially cotton leaf curl virus where endemic; use of certified, treated, disease-free seed; crop rotation with non-host crops to reduce soil-borne disease buildup, particularly important for wilt diseases that can persist in soil for years; control of insect vectors, particularly whitefly for viral diseases; balanced fertilization avoiding excessive nitrogen, which promotes dense, humid canopies favorable to fungal and bacterial disease development; and regular field monitoring to detect disease early.

MAJOR COTTON PESTS
1. Bollworms (a complex of species including American bollworm/Helicoverpa armigera, pink bollworm/Pectinophora gossypiella, and spotted bollworm) - caterpillar pests that feed on squares, flowers, and bolls, causing direct damage to the yield-forming structures of the plant; among the most economically damaging cotton pests historically, driving the widespread adoption of Bt cotton varieties bred to express resistance to certain bollworm species, though pink bollworm in particular has developed resistance to some Bt traits in various regions over time, requiring integrated management approaches.
2. Whitefly (Bemisia tabaci) - a small sap-sucking insect that damages the plant directly through feeding and, more significantly, acts as the vector for cotton leaf curl virus in regions where the disease is present; whitefly populations tend to build up rapidly in hot, dry conditions and can also cause sooty mold development on honeydew excreted onto leaves, reducing photosynthesis.
3. Jassids/leafhoppers - sap-sucking insects that cause a characteristic yellowing/reddening and downward curling ("hopperburn") of leaf margins, reducing photosynthetic capacity and, in severe infestations, causing significant vegetative and yield loss.
4. Aphids - sap-sucking insects that cluster on new growth and the underside of leaves, weakening the plant, excreting honeydew that promotes sooty mold, and in some cases contributing to virus transmission.
5. Thrips - small insects that feed on young leaves and growing points, causing leaf distortion and silvering, particularly damaging to young seedlings.
6. Mealybugs - sap-sucking insects that can infest stems, leaves, and bolls, particularly in some regions where they have emerged as a significant pest, causing stunting, leaf curling, and honeydew-related sooty mold; often spread partly through ants that protect them in exchange for honeydew.
7. Mites (particularly spider mites) - very small pests that cause stippling, yellowing (bronzing), and eventual leaf drop under heavy infestation, generally favored by hot, dry conditions.
Management principles: Integrated Pest Management (IPM) approaches combining regular field scouting to detect pest buildup before economic thresholds are reached; use of resistant/tolerant varieties, including Bt cotton for bollworm management where appropriate, along with mandated non-Bt refuge planting to slow resistance development; conserving natural enemies (predatory insects, parasitic wasps) that help suppress pest populations naturally; avoiding excessive nitrogen application, which promotes lush growth attractive to sucking pests; timely sowing and harvesting to reduce the overlap between vulnerable crop stages and peak pest activity; and judicious, threshold-based use of insecticides with attention to selecting products that minimize harm to natural enemies and pollinators, and rotating modes of action to slow the development of insecticide resistance in pest populations.

SQUARE AND BOLL SHEDDING
Cotton plants naturally shed a significant proportion of the squares, flowers, and young bolls they initially set, a normal physiological process, but excessive shedding beyond the natural level reduces yield and can result from several stress factors including water stress (both drought and waterlogging), extreme temperatures (particularly prolonged high temperatures during flowering), nutrient imbalances (especially boron deficiency and excessive nitrogen leading to excessive vegetative competition), and pest damage (particularly sucking pests and bollworms feeding directly on squares and young bolls). Managing shedding involves maintaining balanced, timely irrigation and fertilization, addressing micronutrient deficiencies where present, and controlling relevant pests, since shedding is often a combined outcome of several interacting stress factors rather than a single cause.

PICKING AND HARVEST
Cotton bolls do not mature simultaneously across the plant or field, since flowering and boll set occur progressively over an extended period; as a result, picking is generally carried out in multiple rounds (commonly three or more) over several weeks to months, harvesting only bolls that have fully matured and opened at each round, rather than a single harvest event as with many grain crops. Hand picking, still widely used in many regions, allows selective picking of only mature, opened bolls and tends to produce cleaner lint with less trash contamination, but is labor-intensive. Mechanical picking (using spindle pickers or strippers) is faster and less labor-intensive but requires more uniform boll opening across the field, which is sometimes promoted through the use of a chemical defoliant/boll opener applied late in the season to remove leaves and encourage more even, complete boll opening before mechanical harvest. Timely picking as bolls open is important to minimize losses and quality deterioration from prolonged field exposure to rain, dew, wind, and soil contamination, all of which can lower lint grade and market value. Picked seed cotton should be kept clean and dry, with prompt transport to ginning facilities to minimize quality loss from moisture absorption, contamination, or extended storage of unginned seed cotton.

GINNING AND POST-HARVEST PROCESSING
After picking, seed cotton is processed at a ginning facility, where the ginning process mechanically separates the lint (fiber) from the cottonseed. The lint is baled for sale to the textile industry, while cottonseed is processed further to extract cottonseed oil (used as edible oil) and cottonseed cake/meal (used as a protein-rich livestock feed ingredient), with the remaining seed hulls and linters having various industrial uses. Fiber quality assessed at or after ginning includes staple length (fiber length, a major determinant of price and spinning suitability), fiber strength, fineness (micronaire value, related to fiber maturity and thickness), color grade, and trash/contamination content, all of which are affected by variety, growing conditions, picking practices, and post-harvest handling.

CROP ROTATION AND CROPPING SYSTEMS
Cotton is commonly rotated with other crops such as wheat, maize, sugarcane, or legume/pulse crops depending on the region and cropping calendar. Crop rotation helps reduce the buildup of soil-borne diseases such as Fusarium and Verticillium wilts, which can persist in soil for extended periods under continuous cotton cultivation, and helps manage certain soil-dwelling pests and weed populations associated with continuous cotton monocropping. Rotating with legume crops can also help improve soil nitrogen status ahead of the following crop. In many systems, cotton is grown as the main kharif (summer) crop, with the field then prepared for a rabi (winter) crop such as wheat after the cotton picking season concludes, requiring careful timing of the final cotton pickings and field clearance to allow timely sowing of the following crop.

MECHANIZATION IN COTTON PRODUCTION
Cotton production can involve mechanization at several stages, though the degree varies considerably by region and farm scale: tractor-drawn implements for land preparation (plowing, harrowing, ridging); precision planters for accurate seed placement, spacing, and depth, particularly important given cotton's sensitivity to plant population; sprayers for herbicide, fungicide, and insecticide application; and, in some large-scale production systems, mechanical spindle pickers or strippers for harvest, often used in conjunction with chemical defoliation to promote uniform boll opening suited to machine picking. In many smallholder and labor-abundant production regions, however, hand picking remains the dominant harvest method due to its ability to selectively pick only mature bolls and produce cleaner lint, despite being more labor-intensive than mechanical picking.

ECONOMIC AND INDUSTRIAL IMPORTANCE
Cotton is one of the most economically important non-food agricultural commodities globally, serving as the primary natural fiber for the textile industry and supporting extensive value chains from farming through ginning, spinning, weaving, and garment manufacturing. Beyond fiber, cottonseed is a valuable by-product, processed into cooking oil and high-protein animal feed (cottonseed cake/meal), adding significant additional value to the crop beyond lint alone. In many cotton-growing regions, the crop is a major source of rural income and employment, both in cultivation and in associated processing and manufacturing industries, and it is also a significant export commodity for a number of producing countries. Cotton stalks and other residues remaining after harvest can additionally be used as fuel or incorporated into soil as organic matter, though residue management is also important for reducing the carryover of certain pests and diseases into the following season.
