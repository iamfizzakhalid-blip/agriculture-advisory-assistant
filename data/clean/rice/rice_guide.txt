Rice, known scientifically as Oryza sativa for the most widely cultivated species, is a semi-aquatic annual grass and one of the most important staple food crops in the world, feeding more than half of the global population as a primary source of calories, especially across Asia. Unlike maize, rice is a C3 plant rather than a C4 plant, which means it photosynthesizes somewhat less efficiently in very hot conditions and has different water and nutrient dynamics, but its defining biological feature is its strong adaptation to growing in standing water, something very few other major food crops can tolerate. The plant has a fibrous root system, hollow jointed stems called culms, long narrow leaves with a small membranous structure called a ligule where the leaf blade meets the sheath, and it produces its grain in a branched flower cluster at the top of the stem called a panicle, which holds many individual spikelets, each of which can develop into a single grain of rice enclosed in a tough outer husk. Rice is primarily self-pollinating, meaning each flower typically fertilizes itself rather than depending on wind or insects to bring in pollen from another plant, which is a significant practical difference from maize, since it makes rice varieties much easier to keep pure from one season to the next and reduces the risk of unwanted cross-pollination between different varieties grown nearby.

The life cycle of rice is generally divided into three broad phases: the vegetative phase, the reproductive phase, and the ripening phase. The vegetative phase begins with germination and seedling establishment and continues through active tillering, which is the process by which the young rice plant sends up multiple additional stems, called tillers, from its base, and the number of productive tillers a plant produces has a major influence on how many panicles, and therefore how much grain, the plant will ultimately produce. This phase ends around what is called panicle initiation, the point at which the growing point inside the stem switches from producing more leaves to beginning to form the young panicle. The reproductive phase then covers panicle development, the emergence of the panicle from the leaf sheath known as booting and heading, and flowering, during which the tiny flowers open briefly and self-pollinate; this flowering stage, much like silking in maize, is highly sensitive to stress, and extreme heat, cold, or drought at this exact point can cause a high percentage of spikelets to fail to set grain, directly reducing yield. After flowering, the plant enters the ripening phase, during which the fertilized grains fill with starch, passing through a watery stage, then a milky stage, then a dough stage, before finally reaching physiological maturity, at which point the grains have hardened and the plant's leaves and stems typically begin to turn golden yellow, which is the visual cue farmers use to judge that the crop is nearing harvest time.

Climate requirements for rice center around warmth and, for most varieties, an ample and reliable water supply. Rice grows best with average temperatures somewhere between about twenty and thirty five degrees Celsius, and it is generally more heat tolerant than many other cereal crops, though very high temperatures during flowering, above around thirty five degrees Celsius, can cause sterility and poor grain set in the same way that heat stress damages maize during silking. Cold temperatures, particularly below about twenty degrees Celsius during flowering, can also cause spikelet sterility in many varieties, which is a significant concern in higher altitude or higher latitude rice growing regions. Because so much rice is grown under flooded conditions, water requirement is unusually high compared to most other cereals, with irrigated lowland rice commonly needing somewhere in the range of one thousand to one thousand five hundred millimeters of water or more across the season when field flooding is included, though this varies enormously depending on soil type, climate, and the specific water management system used. Rice does need good sunlight for strong yields, but it is somewhat more shade tolerant during parts of its growth than maize is.

Soil and water management is really where rice diverges most sharply from most other major crops, because the majority of the world's rice is grown as lowland or paddy rice, meaning the field is deliberately flooded with a shallow layer of standing water for most of the growing season. This standing water suppresses many weeds, moderates soil temperature, and supplies the plant with water reliably, and rice has special internal air channels called aerenchyma that allow oxygen to move down from the leaves to the roots even when the roots are sitting in waterlogged, oxygen poor soil, which is exactly the kind of condition that would kill most other crops, including maize, within a day or two. Because of this reliance on flooding, lowland rice is generally grown on heavier soils with good water holding capacity, often clay or clay loam soils that can hold a standing water layer without excessive seepage, and fields are typically leveled very precisely and surrounded by small earthen bunds to keep irrigation water contained. Rice can also be grown without flooding, in what is called upland or aerobic rice, generally on well drained soils similar to those used for other cereals, relying on rainfall or limited irrigation rather than standing water, though yields in upland systems are usually lower than in well managed flooded systems since the crop does not have the same buffer against water stress. Ideal soil pH for rice is roughly five point five to seven, though rice is notably more tolerant of both acidic and waterlogged, low oxygen soil conditions than most other major crops, which is part of why it can be productively grown in low lying deltas and floodplains that would be unsuitable for crops like maize or wheat.

There are several important classifications of rice varieties that matter to how it is grown and used. In terms of grain type, rice is often divided into long grain, medium grain, and short grain varieties, with long grain rice, such as basmati or jasmine types, generally cooking up light and separate, while short and medium grain varieties tend to be stickier when cooked, a property valued in many East Asian cuisines and in dishes like risotto or sushi. Rice is also classified botanically into two major subspecies, indica and japonica, with indica types being the long, slender grained varieties traditionally grown across most of tropical and subtropical Asia, and japonica types being the shorter, roundish grained varieties more associated with temperate regions like Japan, Korea, and parts of China, though both are now grown in many places worldwide. In terms of farming system, rice is grown as lowland or paddy rice under flooded conditions, as upland or aerobic rice under non-flooded, more rainfed conditions, as deepwater rice in flood prone areas where the plant must be able to elongate rapidly to keep its top above rising floodwater, and as rainfed lowland rice, which sits somewhere between fully irrigated paddy systems and purely rainfed upland systems. As with maize, farmers also choose between open pollinated or traditional varieties, which breed true and allow seed saving, modern high yielding varieties developed through conventional breeding, and hybrid rice, which like hybrid maize is created by crossing specific parent lines to achieve higher yield through hybrid vigor but does not breed true, meaning seed saved from a hybrid crop will not perform as well if replanted, so farmers growing hybrid rice generally need to buy fresh seed each season, similar to the situation with hybrid maize.

Land preparation for lowland rice differs noticeably from that of most dryland crops because it typically involves puddling, a process in which the field is flooded and then plowed or harrowed while wet, which breaks down soil structure into a smooth, compacted, nearly impermeable layer that helps the field hold standing water with minimal seepage and also helps suppress weeds by burying weed seeds and creating an oxygen poor environment they cannot easily germinate in. Before puddling, the field is usually plowed once while relatively dry to incorporate crop residue and any organic amendments like farmyard manure or compost, and the field is carefully leveled, since even, precise leveling is especially important in rice because uneven ground leads to some areas being too deeply flooded and others not flooded enough, both of which hurt yield. Small earthen bunds are built or maintained around the field edges to hold the irrigation water in place. For upland or aerobic rice systems, land preparation looks much more like preparation for other cereal crops, involving plowing and harrowing to create a good seedbed without any deliberate flooding or puddling step.

Rice can be established in the field in a few different ways. Transplanting is the traditional and still very widely used method in much of Asia, in which rice seed is first sown densely in a separate nursery bed, allowed to grow into young seedlings over roughly three to four weeks, and then those seedlings are manually or mechanically pulled up and transplanted individually into the puddled, flooded main field at a spacing of roughly fifteen to twenty centimeters between plants, though this varies by variety and region; this method gives the crop a head start against weeds and allows more precise control of plant spacing, but it is labor intensive. Direct seeding, by contrast, involves sowing rice seed straight into the main field rather than through a nursery, either by broadcasting seed onto a flooded or saturated field, or by drilling seed into a drier, prepared seedbed similar to how other cereals are sown, and this method saves labor and water in some systems but often requires more intensive weed control since the crop does not get the same head start that transplanted seedlings have. Seed rate varies a great deal depending on the establishment method, with transplanted systems typically needing far less seed per unit area than direct seeded systems, since transplanting allows very efficient, deliberate spacing while direct seeding usually oversows to compensate for uneven germination.

Nutrient management in rice follows broadly similar principles to maize but with some important differences related to the flooded growing environment. Nitrogen is again the most critical nutrient and the one most likely to limit yield, with typical total applications for high yielding irrigated rice often falling somewhere around one hundred to one hundred fifty kilograms per hectare, though this varies by variety, yield target, and region, and as with maize it is usually split into multiple doses over the season, commonly at or shortly after transplanting, at active tillering, and again around panicle initiation, both to match the plant's nutrient demand at each stage and because nitrogen can be lost quite readily from flooded rice soils through processes like ammonia volatilization and leaching, so timing and placement of nitrogen fertilizer noticeably affects how efficiently the plant actually uses it. Nitrogen deficiency in rice shows up as an overall pale, yellowish green color in older leaves and stunted growth, quite similar in principle to the deficiency symptoms seen in maize. Phosphorus is important for root development and early tillering, and is typically applied as a basal dose before or at transplanting, since like in maize it does not move readily through the soil; a phosphorus deficient rice plant often shows dark green, sometimes purplish, stunted leaves. Potassium supports stalk strength, disease resistance, and grain filling, much as it does in maize, and deficiency appears as yellowing or browning starting at the leaf tips and margins of older leaves. Zinc deficiency is also very common in flooded rice soils specifically because flooding tends to reduce the availability of zinc to the plant, and it shows up as stunted growth along with brownish, rusty colored spots or streaks on the leaves, a condition sometimes called khaira disease by farmers in parts of South Asia, and it is typically corrected with zinc sulfate application either to the soil or as a foliar spray.

Water management for lowland rice, beyond the general need for a large total water supply, generally involves maintaining a shallow standing water layer, often somewhere around two to five centimeters deep, for most of the vegetative and reproductive period, since continuous shallow flooding tends to support both good yields and strong weed suppression. However, water is often deliberately drained for short periods at certain points, such as briefly around active tillering to encourage stronger root growth, and again before harvest to allow the field to dry out enough for easy harvesting, whether by hand or machine. As with maize, the flowering period is the single most water sensitive stage, and allowing a flooded rice field to dry out and stress the crop right around flowering can cause serious losses in grain set, so this is generally the point at which water management is most carefully protected. In many regions, a water saving technique called alternate wetting and drying has become popular, in which the field is allowed to dry down to a certain point before being reflooded, rather than being kept continuously flooded throughout the season, and this can significantly reduce total water use without a major yield penalty if managed carefully, though it requires more active monitoring than simply keeping the field flooded at all times.

Weed management in rice is closely tied to water management, since maintaining standing water is itself one of the most effective ways to suppress weeds by starving them of the oxygen and light most weed seeds need to germinate and establish. In transplanted systems, the head start given to rice seedlings over weeds, combined with standing water, generally keeps weed pressure more manageable than in direct seeded systems, where weeds can germinate and grow alongside the rice from the very start, making direct seeded rice fields generally more dependent on herbicides or manual weeding to stay clean. Both pre-emergence and post-emergence herbicides are used in rice, chosen to match local weed populations and applied carefully to avoid crop injury, and manual or mechanical weeding, particularly during the first several weeks after establishment, remains common practice in many smallholder systems.

A number of pests affect rice production. The rice stem borer, whose larvae tunnel into the stem, is one of the most damaging pests worldwide, causing a symptom called dead heart in young plants, where the central shoot dies, and a symptom called whitehead in older plants, where the developing panicle turns white and produces no grain because its vascular connection to the rest of the plant has been severed. The brown planthopper is another major pest, a small sap sucking insect that can build up to enormous populations very quickly under the right conditions and cause a distinctive symptom known as hopperburn, where large patches of the field suddenly turn brown and die as if scorched, and it can also transmit certain viral diseases. Rice leaf folder larvae fold and roll rice leaves and feed inside them, reducing the leaf area available for photosynthesis. Gall midge causes an abnormal tubular growth on the plant called a silver shoot, which produces no grain. Rice bugs feed directly on developing grains, causing discolored or empty kernels. As with maize, birds and rodents can also cause meaningful losses, particularly as the crop nears maturity and the grain becomes attractive to them, and integrated pest management, relying on regular field monitoring, resistant varieties, conservation of natural enemies like spiders and parasitic wasps that are often abundant in flooded rice systems, and targeted rather than routine pesticide use, is generally the recommended approach.

Diseases are also a major concern in rice. Rice blast, caused by a fungus, is one of the most economically damaging rice diseases worldwide and can affect leaves, stems, and the panicle itself, appearing as diamond shaped lesions with gray centers on leaves, and if it infects the neck of the panicle it can cause the entire panicle to die and produce little or no grain; management relies heavily on resistant varieties along with fungicide use in severe cases. Bacterial leaf blight causes water soaked streaks along the leaf margins that turn yellow and then gray as the leaf dies back from the tip, and it spreads readily in warm, humid, windy conditions. Sheath blight, another fungal disease, produces irregular greenish gray lesions on the leaf sheath near the waterline and can spread up the plant under dense, heavily fertilized crop canopies. Various viral diseases, such as tungro, are transmitted by insect vectors like leafhoppers and cause stunting and yellow or orange discoloration of the leaves, and since there is no direct cure once a plant is infected, management again focuses on controlling the insect vectors and using resistant varieties where available. Grain discoloration, sometimes linked to fungal infection during grain filling, can also reduce both the yield and the market quality of the harvested rice.

In terms of harvesting, rice is generally harvested when the grains have reached physiological maturity, which farmers commonly judge by the color of the crop, watching for the majority of the panicles and the straw beneath them to turn from green to a golden yellow color, and by grain moisture, with harvest typically targeted somewhere around twenty to twenty five percent grain moisture, similar to the target range for maize. Harvesting too early results in a higher proportion of unfilled or chalky grains and lower overall yield, while harvesting too late increases losses from grains shattering out of the panicle and falling to the ground, as well as increasing vulnerability to lodging, birds, and pests. Harvesting can be done by hand with sickles, which remains common in many smallholder systems, particularly where field conditions are difficult for machinery, or by mechanical combine harvester, which cuts, threshes, and cleans the grain in a single pass and is increasingly common in larger, more mechanized rice growing regions.

After harvest, freshly harvested rice, much like maize, needs to be dried down promptly, typically to around twelve to fourteen percent moisture, to prevent mold growth, insect infestation, and quality deterioration during storage; this drying can be done by spreading the grain out in the sun on a hard, clean surface, which is common in many smallholder systems, or through mechanical dryers in more industrialized operations. Threshing, meaning separating the grains from the panicle and straw, is done either as part of a combine harvester's operation or as a separate step afterward using a mechanical thresher or, in some smallholder systems, by beating harvested bundles against a hard surface. The harvested grain at this stage is called paddy or rough rice, still encased in its tough outer husk, and this husk is removed through a milling process to produce brown rice, and if the bran layer is further polished away, white rice; milling and storage conditions both significantly affect the final quality, breakage rate, and shelf life of the rice that eventually reaches consumers.

Rice is used overwhelmingly as a direct human food staple, eaten as whole cooked grain in an enormous range of dishes across nearly every rice growing culture, and it is also processed into rice flour, rice noodles, rice paper, breakfast cereals, and fermented products like rice wine in various regional cuisines. The husk and bran removed during milling, while not eaten directly by most people, have their own uses, with rice bran sometimes pressed for oil and both husk and bran commonly used as animal feed ingredients or as fuel or organic matter in some systems. Rice straw, the leftover stems and leaves after harvest, is used in many regions as animal fodder, as material for thatching or crafts, or is incorporated back into the soil or, in some places, burned, though residue burning has become an increasing environmental and air quality concern in several major rice growing regions.

From a farming and economic standpoint, the major cost inputs in rice production are seed, particularly for farmers using hybrid varieties who need to purchase new seed each season, fertilizer, the labor and water costs associated with land preparation, transplanting, and irrigation, which tend to be higher in rice than in most dryland cereals precisely because of the flooding and, where used, transplanting steps, and crop protection inputs like pesticides and herbicides. Yield potential varies widely with management, water availability, and variety, with rainfed or upland systems with minimal inputs often producing somewhere around one and a half to three tons per hectare, while well managed irrigated systems using modern high yielding or hybrid varieties can commonly exceed six to eight tons per hectare and sometimes considerably more under intensive management. Because rice fields are so often flooded for extended periods, continuous rice cultivation can also lead to certain soil and pest management challenges over time, so many farming systems rotate rice with a dryland crop, such as wheat in parts of South Asia or various legumes and vegetables elsewhere, both to break pest and disease cycles that build up under continuous flooding and to make more diverse use of the land and water available across the year. As with maize, market prices for rice tend to be lowest right around regional harvest time when supply is highest, so farmers who are able to dry and store their grain safely often have the option of selling later in the season, once prices have had a chance to recover from the immediate post-harvest glut.
